==============================================
Fazzari on Geometrography & Legacy (pp. X–XVI)
==============================================

.. folio:: 0023
   :page: X

Cardano (6), Tartaglia (7), and Benedetti (8), sixteenth-century Italian geometers, proposed and succeeded in constructing all Euclidean problems—that is, those of the first and second degree—by means of the straightedge and the compass of constant opening, a geometry moreover not unknown to the Greeks, as appears from a passage of Pappus of Alexandria in his *Mathematical Collection*,
and which had already aroused the interest of the Arabs, as appears from a *Book of Geometric Constructions* by Abū al-Wafā' (9); Poncelet (10) and Steiner (11) then proved that these problems can likewise be constructed with the straightedge and a fixed circle of known center on the drawing sheet. Brianchon (12), instead, at the beginning of the nineteenth century cultivated another branch of Geometry, proposing the solution of certain problems by the use of the straightedge alone.
Before Brianchon, however, Schooten (13), Lambert (14), Servois (15), and our Mascheroni in the work mentioned earlier (see no. 9) occupied themselves with the *Geometry of the straightedge*, having especially in view the practical problems that concern surveyors and artillerymen—problems proposing to perform various geometric operations in the field, such as *measuring distances*, *drawing parallels*, etc.
Moreover, the *Geometry of the straightedge*, which connects with *Projective Geometry*—giving prominence among all lines to the *straight line*—was widely cultivated in the century just ended by a host of able geometers of every country (16).

Coatpont also, in an article published in 1877 in the *Nouvelle correspondance mathématique*, conceived the straightedge under another aspect.

He says: "I define a *rule* as a *blade* presenting two rectilinear and parallel edges, enabling one to draw equidistant parallels," thus founding a new branch of Geometry, the *Geometry of the flat ruler* (17). We cannot here, given the limits we have set ourselves, give fuller information about these branches of Geometry,
yet considering the usefulness that the teaching of elementary mathematics in secondary schools in particular would derive from such studies, we express the hope that some able cultivator of the geometric disciplines may write a work for our young students, from which they may learn the constructions of Euclidean problems with the various instruments that can be used.

.. folio:: 0024
   :page: XI

In this work—admired even by that by no means easy admirer, Steiner—Mascheroni solves, by using the compass alone, all Euclidean problems with ingenious constructions, and further solves with the greatest approximation various problems of an order higher than elementary Geometry properly so called, which require the use of curves other than the circumference.

If his demonstrations, with their continual appeal to the propositions of Euclid—moreover the sole textbook of elementary Geometry at that time—sometimes appear heavy and could nowadays be simplified, his constructions are always most elegant and often simpler than those ordinarily performed using both straightedge and compass: of this the reader can easily convince himself by applying, for example,
the method of *Lemoine's Geometrography* (18) to determine the simplicity and accuracy of a geometric construction. And "there is no learned geometer who even today, after nearly a century of advances in this science, cannot study Mascheroni's work without reaping good fruit (19)."

Mascheroni dedicated the volume to *Bonaparte l'italico* with a short poem, and presented it to him at Mombello, where Napoleon, having defeated the enemy, had withdrawn on May 17, 1797, intent on organizing the Cisalpine Republic.

The young General, always eager to astonish the public in small things as in great, wished himself to communicate the new geometric constructions
to Laplace and Lagrange, as we read in the *Moniteur* of 20 Ventôse, Year VI (March 10, 1798; no. 170, p. 684) on the occasion of the announcement of the French translation of this work made by Antoine-Michel Carette: "The French geometers had not yet been able to obtain this learned work of Mascheroni, already celebrated in the career of the mathematical sciences through a profound commentary on Euler's integral calculus.
Bonaparte made it known about two months ago to Laplace and Lagrange, who formed the most favorable opinion of this new kind of geometry." In the same journal is noted the wonder aroused on December 11, 1797, at the Institute by Bonaparte, who proposed to those mathematicians certain problems from the *Geometry of the Compass*,
and we read that Laplace could give no other answer than: "We expected everything from you, General, except lessons in mathematics."

.. folio:: 0025
   :page: XII

The celebrated mathematician and astronomer Delambre, perpetual secretary of the French Institute for the exact sciences, in his general report to the Emperor Napoleon on the progress of mathematics from 1789 to 1808, though sparing in praise of foreigners, referring to Mascheroni's work expresses himself thus:
"Ancient Geometry admitted in its demonstrations only what can be executed by means of the straightedge and the compass. Mascheroni, stricter still, chose to exclude the straightedge. One must certainly marvel greatly at the large number of new and very acute propositions that he knew how to find in an apparently exhausted subject.
His principal theorems had been brought to France with the Treaty of Campoformio by the conqueror and pacifier of Italy. A desire arose among French scholars to know Mascheroni's work in full, and the translation was soon published."

The French translation was made, as we mentioned above, by Carette and published in Paris first in 1798 and a second time in 1828. In Germany as well a German translation was made by Güson, published in Berlin in 1825.

The bibliography of the *Geometry of the Compass* is by no means rich.
Besides the two editions of the French translation and the edition of the German translation of Mascheroni's work, we have several reworkings of the same work (20) and two notes—one by Dubouis in the *Journal de Math.* (t. XXII, 1892) and the other by Professor G. Cesàro in the *Mémoires de la société royale des sciences* of *Liège*—in which the possibility of solving elementary problems with the compass alone is proved without knowing Mascheroni's work.
We then have a brief study by Adler (21), who proves the same by means of the principle of transformation by reciprocal radius vectors. A noteworthy application of Mascheroni's method is the division of the circumference into 17 equal parts recently obtained by Gérard (22) with the compass alone, a division that Klein already declared *certainly possible* (23).
One cannot therefore say that "the researches carried out by the author of the *Geometry of the Compass* have given geometers much occasion to press on to further studies on the subject. This is a pity, because this kind of research could not only lead to interesting results as a scientific curiosity,
but could also render good service in the field of instruction" (24). Perhaps the rarity of the edition of Mascheroni's work contributed to this, and it is for this reason that we were moved to republish it.

   :page: XIII

12. Let us continue the enumeration of Mascheroni's works. *General Notices of the New System of Weights and Measures Deduced from the Magnitude of the Earth* (Milan, 1798, in-8vo).

Cesare Beccaria as early as January 25, 1780, had presented a report to the Cameral Magistrate for the State of Milan on the uniformity of measures of length, proposing to divide these measures into decimal fractions; yet the proposal of the author of the celebrated book *On Crimes and Punishments* remained unheeded
until the legislative council of the Cisalpine Republic on March 11, 1798, approved the election of a special Commission to establish a decimal system of weights, measures, and coins analogous to that of the French Republic. Among the Commissioners was Mascheroni, who, to make the new system acceptable to the public by explaining its full practical utility,
published the said pamphlet, which earned him the honor of being called among the delegates of the French Republic and the allied or neutral States on the Commission that was to establish the new common measures; an honor he paid for with his death.

13. In vol. IX of the *Memorie di Matematica e Fisica della Società Italiana delle scienze* (Modena, 1802) is inserted a posthumous work of Mascheroni entitled
*Popular Explanation of the Manner in Which the Leap or Intercalary Year and the Beginning of the Republican Year Are Regulated*, which he had sent on 24 Germinal Year VII (April 13, 1799) to Serbelloni, ambassador of the Cisalpine Republic to the French Republic.

Of this study Reullier wrote thus to Serbelloni: "This work appeared to me infinitely well suited to make familiar these naturally abstract ideas. In my opinion it cannot fail to be very useful to Italy, and to do honor to its Author."

----

After Mascheroni's death, his numerous manuscripts existing in Paris were consigned to his brother Giuseppe and sold by him in 1819 to the learned bibliophile, the advocate Aloisio Fantoni of Rovetta, who with venerable affection arranged them, binding them into volumes. Upon Fantoni's death in 1874, his heirs offered them for sale, and Dr. Vincenzo Barca made the purchase;
on the latter's death the precious volumes passed to his heir, Signora Chiarina Barca-Albani.

.. folio:: 0027
   :page: XIV

These manuscripts were, at the invitation of Pancaldi, Minister of Foreign Affairs of the Cisalpine Republic, first examined by the celebrated astronomer of the Milan Observatory, Abbé Barnaba Oriani, who expressed the hope that the two studies containing
a *Treatise on the Measurement of Triangular Pyramids* and a *Memoir on the Integration of Certain Formulas by Means of Convergent Series* might be published, since "these works would serve ever more to shed luster on the name of a man who honored his Homeland and Italy with his talents" (25).

Ravelli in his *Bibliography* lists the writings of this great collection, which comprises "fully 38 autograph volumes, partly in folio, or in-4to or in-8vo," as well as the list of other autographs of Mascheroni. Repeatedly wishes were expressed that these writings be published, yet hitherto in vain, notwithstanding that the illustrious Prof. P. Riccardi in the note mentioned above wrote as early as 1886:
"Among the mathematical works, now centuries old, published by Italian authors, which still retain an absolute scientific and literary merit and an uncommon bibliographic worth, are to be numbered those of the most able geometer Lorenzo Mascheroni." And may it also be permitted to us once again to express the hope that at last the deserved tribute of gratitude be rendered to Mascheroni's work
by publishing his unpublished writings.

He who so greatly honored his homeland has the right that his fellow citizens not leave in oblivion the fruit of his genius.

Several of Mascheroni's mathematical works are cited among the standard linguistic texts in the Vocabolario della Crusca, and among them is included the *Geometry of the Compass*.

Before concluding these notes on the mathematical works of Mascheroni, we feel it our duty to render heartfelt thanks to the illustrious Doctor A. Monti, Professor at the University of Pavia, who not only supplied us with bibliographic data, but kindly sent us a photograph of Mascheroni, from which is taken the likeness of the Bergamasque mathematician seen in this reprint.
This photograph is taken from the portrait of the Author found at the front of his eulogy written by Marquis Ferdinando Landi in 1804, reproduced again in the volume that Prof. Fiammazzo published on the occasion of the first centenary of the death of Lorenzo Mascheroni.

**Prof. Gaetano Fazzari.**
