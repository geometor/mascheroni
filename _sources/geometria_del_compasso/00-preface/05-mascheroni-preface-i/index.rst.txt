**PREFACE**

The first thought that invited me to try the new paths of this *Geometry of the Compass* was this: while so many new things are discovered by advancing in mathematics, might one not perhaps find some still unknown place by turning back?
Hitherto the simplest solutions of geometry have been judged to be those which employ nothing other than the compass and the straightedge; that is, what is the same, the straight line, which is the simplest among lines, and the circle, which is the simplest among curves.
To these two instruments, so to speak, of problems, which once determined and constituted elementary geometry, conic sections were added in due course; then curves above the second degree and transcendentals of various kinds.
Men have continued to enrich these profound geometric investigations with the new aids of algebra, both finite and infinitesimal, to such a degree that those discoveries which at first seemed marvelous to the ancients, and worthy of the sacrifices of Thales and Pythagoras, are now the patrimony of the children of our days.
Now I said: could you not turn back from the elements, as from a boundary line, and seek something left behind as if neglected? Is it true that the elementary problems of Euclid are of the simplest construction?
Or could not the mathematical element be resolved into its fundamental elements, straightedge and compass, after the manner of one who has separated water into two airs, and some air even esteemed simple into two other substances?
At this point I perceived that, whereas one cannot make use of the straightedge alone except to draw a straight line, one could perhaps make use of the compass alone not merely to describe a circle or an arc thereof,
but by describing several with several centers and various openings, to find by means of their mutual intersections several points that might be useful, and precisely those sought in position in some problem.

So far I recognized that this was a branch hitherto utterly uncultivated by mathematicians, and that solutions of this kind obtained by

.. folio:: 0033
   :page: 4

chance
with the compass alone would be by their construction more elementary than any other. Yet two things held me back for a little while from setting out to attempt anything along this path.
The first was the thought: what profit will come if you succeed in finding with the compass alone those points which others have already hitherto found with it and with the straightedge?
The second was the fear, which at first seemed to me very reasonable, that rather than gaining advantage from my attempts, even were they crowned with success, I should suffer loss. Constructions with the compass alone for finding the points of elementary geometry would have been many times more complicated than those already known, in which the straightedge intervenes.
Theory would thus have lacked elegance, and practice precision. So I was on the point of abandoning the enterprise.

While I was thus irresolute, I happened to reread the manner in which Graham and Bird divided their great astronomical quadrants in England (*Encyclop. Méthod.*, article *quart de cercle mural*).
Graham's quadrant, made by him for Greenwich, is not only said to have served as a model for most of those made afterward, but is still regarded by astronomers, for its precision, as one of the best ever employed in astronomy up to the era of Ramsden's quadrants.
Now I saw that the division of that celebrated machine, the straightedge having been wholly abandoned, was executed with the compass alone. Interesting is the description of the method employed in that long and ingenious operation. I will not enter here into stating the reasons for which the straightedge was excluded from it.
Those who have skill in works of this kind will easily guess them. To indicate in general the advantages which the compass has over the straightedge, whenever it is a question of an accurate drawing of lines that must not fear examination under the microscope, it suffices to note
that, especially in the case of a somewhat long straightedge, it is almost impossible that it be so straight as to guarantee throughout its whole extent the position in place of the points that lie upon it. And even suppose it were perfectly straight.
Practical men know that having to slide along it with the scribing point brings with it an uncertainty of parallelism in the motion of the axis of that point, or of perfect fitting to the edge, which often renders its utmost perfection useless.
To these two difficulties the compass is not subject. Whenever it is firm in its opening and exceedingly fine at its points, once one point is centered immovably—which is not difficult—the other, sweeping around, marks of itself an arc so precise and exact that nothing could be more so.

.. folio:: 0034
   :page: 5

In reading that description I noticed that Graham met with four inconveniences. The first was that he had to work by trial. Setting aside the arc of sixty degrees, which was determined by him with the radius of the circle, all his subdivisions were executed by trial.
The ancients furnished no means of dividing the circumference of a circle with the compass alone, other than into six; this is set forth and demonstrated in the fifteenth proposition of the fourth book of Euclid. Graham could not therefore obtain geometric precision except at a single point.


The second inconvenience was the loss of time, which is necessarily consumed even by the most skillful in trials.

The third was having to employ two planes: one upon which to make the trials, the other upon which to transfer the results, which was the plane of the quadrant itself.
This he did so as not to mar the surface of the limb with trials upon the quadrant.

The fourth was having to execute two divisions of different kinds. Since the division of the quadrant into ninety degrees carried with it the subdivisions of an arc into three and into five parts,
and the trials of these subdivisions turned out imperfect through excessive accumulation of errors, he resolved to execute another division on the quadrant itself alongside the first, which should proceed solely by way of bisections.
Having therefore divided the arc of sixty degrees into two parts, and obtained the arc of thirty, and thus the quadrant divided into three parts; by subdivisions by two he next had the sixth, then the twelfth part, etc., until the whole quadrant remained divided into ninety-six parts.
Since this was the division that deserved greater confidence, the other division into ninety degrees, which was moreover the one that had immediately to serve astronomers, was compared and corrected against it by means of a table computed for the purpose.


All these inconveniences were perhaps the reason for which Bird resorted to another method for dividing his quadrants. He determined the arcs by means of their chords, which he took from a scale of equal parts. Yet not even this second manner is free from imperfections;
for in the first place it lacks geometric precision, and in the second place it transfers onto the quadrant the inaccuracies that might be found in the scale.

The consideration of the importance of astronomical instruments recalled my mind to look upon my project of the

.. folio:: 0035
   :page: 6

*Geometry of the Compass* from a more favorable point of view.
I began to believe that I would have done much if I could have divided the circumference with the compass alone into more parts than six. The further I could have pushed the subdivision, and the more it agreed with the division of the quadrant into ninety degrees, the greater service I would have rendered to the artificers of astronomy.
I would have procured for them geometric precision; I would have spared them the time of trials, the twofold kind of divisions, the necessity of two planes, and the not altogether reliable and non-geometric use of scales.


There remained for me only the fear that even if this new method should by chance be found, it might prove so complicated and long as to be no longer sufficiently convenient for use. I set myself to the task.
Seeing that the application of algebra to geometry did not greatly assist me in this sort of inquiry, I wandered along other paths almost purely geometric, which I would indicate here if it were helpful;
but since I scarcely kept a constant track in my course, and owe much to chance, which after various wanderings among diverse expedients presented to me, and not always so promptly, the result I desired, I shall say nothing of them.
Perhaps someone else will be able to discern a thread in this doctrine that leads in order from one problem to another, and which, had it been discovered from the beginning, would have facilitated and shortened the invention.


The first specimen of my success I addressed two years ago in a letter inserted in the *Giornale Brugnatelli* to the excellent artist Citizen Annibale Beccaria, then a Milanese patrician, and now a municipal magistrate and fellow of public instruction,
who to being brother of the most celebrated author of *On Crimes and Punishments* adds the true and proper glory of crafting, whenever he pleases, the finest mathematical instruments. That specimen of mine consisted in the method of dividing the circumference into twenty-four parts with the aid of a single point taken outside it.
The construction of this division is the simplest that can be hoped for, and I have retained it; the other, into one hundred and twenty, which I set forth there, was too complicated; I have now found a much shorter one, and such that I believe it to be the shortest possible.
I added to it a ready construction for obtaining the square roots from one up to ten, which I have likewise retained here. The other problems set forth in that letter, as being complicated or of slight approximation, are here omitted.
