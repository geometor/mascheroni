======================================================
Mascheroni's Preface: Architecture of the Twelve Books
======================================================

.. folio:: 0035
   :page: 6

Now I have arrived, as will be seen from the book, at dividing

.. folio:: 0036
   :page: 7

promptly the circumference into two hundred and forty parts with geometric exactness by means of the compass alone and employing nothing else than three points taken outside the circumference itself.
Each of these parts comes out to one degree and a half of the division hitherto used into three hundred and sixty degrees. I divide, whenever it pleases, each arc into two. That geometrically. By way of approximation, I divide the circumference by the sole means of those three points into degrees and quarters of a degree
without the error of a sixth part of a second. With the same three points I also divide into minutes, always remaining below the error of one second. That astronomers may be satisfied with this precision, reading that not even the most celebrated craftsmen have gone further has flattered me into believing.

Yet it did not seem to me that I had done enough if I did not serve with my theories also the new division of the circle. It is known that the French, fortunate in having within the bosom of their republic the foremost geometers in the universe,
seconding their counsels, have at last fulfilled the long desires of scholars by sanctioning in all the arts the decimal division alone. This division—perhaps slowly in other provinces through the clash of prejudices, and still more through the reaction of inertia—
will nevertheless invincibly take root in time wherever any love for the sciences, or a well-understood interest in commerce, has a place. One of the divisions that could not fail to prove most difficult to alter was that of the circumference of the circle,
both on account of the antiquity of the division into 360 and subdivision into 60 received by all nations, and on account of the labor needed to recompute the trigonometric tables in any new system. Yet the energy of a great nation regenerating itself has conquered all.
Four hundred parts or degrees having been fixed in the circumference, so that the quadrant, which is the foundation of trigonometry, remains divided into one hundred, and each of these hundredths subdivided into one hundred, and so on, the tables of their natural and artificial sines have already been calculated and printed;
and so that nothing be wanting to ensure and increase the accuracy of the numbers, the new French discovery of printing with type soldered in lead conspired to aid, and we already have in hand these new tables of that edition called stereotype, executed by Firmin Didot.
Moreover, others very full, with a great number of decimals, are expected, which are being prepared under the direction of the celebrated Prony by a multitude of most active calculators. All this impelled me to seek a method at least of approximation for dividing the circumference into such new

.. folio:: 0037
   :page: 8

degrees and minutes, and I have succeeded by the ministry of those three points alone in obtaining with fairly few turns of the compass these centesimal degrees and minutes without the small error of one second.

Had nothing else been done, the handling of the pure compass would nevertheless have been sufficiently recommended.
Yet along the way I found that there is no problem of elementary geometry that could not be solved with the compass alone; in this sense, namely, of finding all those points that are required in the problem for the position and determination of the straight lines needed in it.
This concerned theory. I wished to exhaust the subject, to supply all the elements for this purpose, and to demonstrate that all the points which Euclid or other elementary writers find with the aid of the compass and the straightedge together can be found by the first instrument alone.


Not all elementary problems solved with the compass alone have a sufficiently simple construction. I venture however to say that the greater part of the most necessary ones are brief and succinct to such a degree
that whoever wishes to solve them in practice will find it better to use the compass alone to find their fundamental points, repudiating the straightedge; the paths I propose in the book will justify what I say.

I will not indicate here all those Problems of this sort that seem to me of some importance. Here are nevertheless a few.
If one wishes to find with the compass alone the center of a circle, one will have it with a few strokes expeditiously. Likewise one will have third and fourth proportionals; I do not say mean proportionals.
Whoever wishes to construct regular polygons not only within or around given circles, but upon given bases, will have the easy means in the compass (1).
Whoever wishes to find square roots of numbers, that is, to duplicate or multiply in any way in area squares, or circles, or similar figures of whatever kind, will do so quickly by means of the compass. And all this with geometric precision, since the nature of such problems admits of it.
By approximation, moreover, whoever wishes to obtain a length equal to the circumference of a circle, or an arc equal to the radius, or a square equal to a circle, or a circle equal to a square, or a cube equal to a sphere, or a sphere equal to a cube, or a cube double another, or triple, or quadruple,
will be able to obtain it by employing intersections of arcs, that is, by always determining with nothing

.. folio:: 0038
   :page: 9

else than the compass the length of those sides or those radii which are needed for the required figures.

(1) *Military architects will perhaps find there many things suited to their uses.*


Such in brief is what forms the **Geometry of the Compass**, which I present to the public. As for the demonstrations, I have striven to make them geometric after the manner of the ancients. This seemed to me more fitting to the nature of my problems, and shorter.
Wherever geometrically they were bound to turn out too long, I have shortened the path by calculation. I have therefore served brevity, clarity, and elegance all at once as much as I could. I have cited Euclid, the great master of the elements.
Where proportions occur, the numbers of the propositions that I cite are those of Tacquet. The reason for this is that this book is more in everyone's hands than the ancient text of Euclid. I have set in larger type the paragraphs that will be of greater service to craftsmen.
Whoever wants the demonstration must read the whole book. Whoever wants only the practical part may omit whatever is printed in smaller type. That is what craftsmen will do; and so will whoever wishes only to amuse himself with the compass. (1)
To this kind of diversion I have assigned many problems drawn from Pappus, from Ozanam, from Simpson, and from others, which I have placed in the eleventh book. That is all I had to say to my readers concerning this **Geometry of the Compass**, which I present to them,
which for the construction of its problems is the simplest and most elementary geometry that can be had, and which hitherto, so far as I know, had been touched by no one.

----

(1) *Since the aim of this reprint is principally didactic, we have thought it best not to make the said distinction in the text, and the whole book is therefore printed in a single body of type.* — **Editor's Note**
