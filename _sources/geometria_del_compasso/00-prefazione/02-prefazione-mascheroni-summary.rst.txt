====================================
Summary: Mascheroni's 1797 Preface
====================================

.. rubric:: Epistemological Decomposition and Instrument Mechanics

.. folio:: 0032

The Chemical Metaphor of Geometry
---------------------------------

Mascheroni introduces a foundational epistemology of mathematical instruments:

1. **Euclid's Compound Element:** Classical plane geometry relies simultaneously on the straightedge (Postulates 1–2) and the compass (Postulate 3).
2. **Decomposition into Elements:** Just as modern pneumatic chemistry (Lavoisier, Cavendish) resolved water into two constituent gases (*due arie*), Mascheroni asks whether Euclidean construction can be resolved into its single purest mechanical constituent: the compass.
3. **The Asymmetry:** The straightedge alone can only produce points collinear with given points. The compass alone, however, produces intersections of circles, encoding distance, angle, and position.

Instrumental Precision: Compass vs. Straightedge
------------------------------------------------

Mascheroni articulates two decisive mechanical advantages of the compass:

* **Geometric Rigidity:** A straightedge across long distances suffers from elastic bending, edge unevenness, and manufacturing imperfections. A pair of fixed-span dividers (*compasso fedele*) maintains an exact rigid radius invariant under rotation.
* **Stylus Error Propagation:** Tracing a stylus along a physical ruler requires constant manual pressure against an edge, causing variable friction, angular wobble, and positional deviation under microscopic examination. A pivoting compass leg rotates with uniform circular precision around a stationary center.

Precedent in Precision Astronomy
--------------------------------

Mascheroni grounds his theory in the practical art of dividing astronomical instruments:

* :person:`George Graham <graham>` (1673–1751) and :person:`John Bird <bird>` (1709–1776) graduated the celebrated 8-foot mural quadrants at Greenwich Observatory using compass bisection and beam dividers, completely discarding the straightedge for division marks.
* :person:`Jesse Ramsden <ramsden>` (1735–1800) mechanized circular dividing engines, reinforcing the principle that circular division exceeds linear ruling in mechanical accuracy.
