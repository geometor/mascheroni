§§ 69–70: Division into Any Number of Equal Parts
=================================================

.. folio:: 0062
   :page: 37

**69.** *Prob.* To divide a distance :math:`AB` into any number of equal parts.

*Sol.* From an example or two the general rule will be better perceived (fig. 21).

.. picture:: 0062.g1.svg
   :alt: Calibrated 5-layer vector diagram of Mascheroni Book III § 69 (Fig. 21): Division into n Equal Parts (Case n = 3)

.. picture:: 0062.g1.png
   :alt: Archival Fig. 21 (folio 0062, p. 37): General n-part compass division scheme

*Ex. I.* Let the distance :math:`AB` be to be divided into five equal parts. To it add in a straight line the four distances equal to it, :math:`AE, EF, FG, GH` (§ 65), so that it remains quintupled in :math:`BH`, that is, multiplied by as many units as the number of parts into which it is to be divided.
With the extremities :math:`B` and :math:`H` as centers, and with radius :math:`AB` of the length of the distance to be divided, describe the two indefinite arcs :math:`AC, GI`. With the same centers :math:`B` and :math:`H`, and with radius :math:`BH`, describe the two arcs :math:`HI, BC`. With center :math:`C` and the first radius :math:`AB` describe an indefinite arc :math:`BQ`.
With radius :math:`CI` and center :math:`H` describe an arc cutting arc :math:`BQ` at :math:`Q`. The distance :math:`BQ` will lie along the direction of :math:`BA`, and will be the fifth part of it.

If now to :math:`BQ` we add the equal :math:`Qq` (§ 64), then the other equals :math:`qr, rs`, all the fifth parts of :math:`BA` will be determined.

.. folio:: 0063
   :page: 38

*Ex. II.* If it is desired to divide :math:`AB` into seven parts (fig. 22);

.. picture:: 0063.g1.svg
   :alt: Calibrated 5-layer vector diagram of Mascheroni Book III § 70 (Fig. 22): Division into n Equal Parts (Extended Array)

.. picture:: 0063.g1.png
   :alt: Archival Fig. 22 (folio 0063, p. 38): Example II — division into 5 equal parts

form :math:`BH` seven times greater than :math:`BA`. With the extremities of this, namely points :math:`B` and :math:`H`, and with radius :math:`AB`, describe the indefinite arcs :math:`AC, GI`.
With the same centers :math:`B` and :math:`H`, and with radius :math:`BH`, describe the two arcs :math:`HI, BC`. With center :math:`C` and the first radius :math:`AB` describe an indefinite arc :math:`BQ`. With radius :math:`CI` and center :math:`H` cut this arc at :math:`Q`; :math:`BQ` will lie along the direction of :math:`BA`, and will be the seventh part of it.

*Dem.* Since triangle :math:`CQI` has sides respectively equal to triangle :math:`IHQ`, angle :math:`CIQ = IQH` (I.8), hence :math:`CI` is parallel to :math:`HQ` (I.28). And since :math:`CI` is also parallel to :math:`BH` (§ 23), point :math:`Q` will lie on :math:`BH`.
Hence the two isosceles triangles :math:`CBQ, CBH` having a common base angle at :math:`B`, they will be similar (I.5 and 32; VI.4), and :math:`HB : BC :: BC : BQ`, or :math:`HB : AB :: AB : BQ`. Therefore as many times as :math:`HB` is greater than :math:`AB`, so many times will :math:`AB` be greater than :math:`BQ`.

*Sol. II.* Let it be desired to divide :math:`AB`, for example, into five parts (fig. 23).

.. picture:: 0063.g2.svg
   :alt: Calibrated 5-layer vector diagram of Mascheroni Book III § 70 (Fig. 23): Symmetric Arcs for n-Division

.. picture:: 0063.g2.png
   :alt: Archival Fig. 23 (folio 0063, p. 38): Alternative n-section via similar chords

Having determined, as in Sol. I, :math:`BH` five times greater than :math:`AB`, with center :math:`H` and radius :math:`HB`

.. folio:: 0064
   :page: 39

describe an indeterminate arc :math:`CBc`.
Now with radius :math:`BA` and center :math:`B` describe the semicircumference :math:`cCK` (§ 64). With the same radius :math:`AB` and center :math:`C` describe the arc :math:`BQ`. With radius :math:`CK` and center :math:`B` cut this arc at :math:`Q`; :math:`BQ` will be the fifth part of :math:`BA` placed along the same direction.

*Dem.* The straight line :math:`BK` will lie on the continuation of :math:`Bc` (IV.15). Making therefore the due substitutions in § 22, we shall have :math:`KC \cdot BH = (BC)^2 = (AB)^2`; and hence (VI.17) :math:`BH : AB :: AB : KC`, or :math:`BH : AB :: AB : BQ`.
As much, therefore, as :math:`BH` is greater than :math:`AB`, so much will :math:`AB` itself be greater than :math:`BQ`; and if :math:`BH = 5\,AB`, then also :math:`AB = 5\,BQ`. Moreover, from the mutual equality of the sides in the two triangles :math:`BKC, BCQ`, we shall have angle :math:`KCB = CBQ` (I.8).
But to angle :math:`KCB` is also equal angle :math:`CBH` (§ 22). Therefore :math:`BQ` lies along the direction of :math:`BH`.

**70.** It is clearly seen that this last Problem (§ 69) can be very useful in practice for dividing into lines a given foot already divided into inches; since by its method, :math:`BH` being equal to twelve inches, :math:`BQ` will turn out equal to one twelfth of the first inch :math:`AB`, that is, to one line.
In the same manner the meter already divided into decimeters can be subdivided into centimeters.
If the straight line :math:`AB`, upon which point :math:`Q` is to be found, has already been drawn, the operation will be simpler, since without describing arc :math:`BQ` with center :math:`C` and radius :math:`AB`, it will suffice to cut the given straight line :math:`AB` at :math:`Q` with the compass openings indicated above.
