§ 1. Dedication to Bonaparte (Pavia, Anno V / 1797)
---------------------------------------------------

In 1797, following the arrival of the French Army of Italy in Lombardy, :person:`Lorenzo Mascheroni <mascheroni>` dedicated *La Geometria del Compasso* (:cite:`mascheroni-1797`) to General :person:`Napoléon Bonaparte <bonaparte>` in hendecasyllabic verse. Bonaparte, trained in the artillery corps at Brienne and the École Militaire and an ardent lover of geometry, embraced Mascheroni's discovery with enthusiasm.

Returning to Paris in December 1797 upon the signing of the Treaty of Campo Formio, Bonaparte took his seat in the *Institut National des Sciences et Arts* (having been elected to the First Class of Physical and Mathematical Sciences). There he presented Mascheroni's compass-only constructions—particularly the quadrisection of a circle using four arcs—to :person:`Pierre-Simon Laplace <laplace>` and :person:`Joseph-Louis Lagrange <lagrange>`, eliciting Laplace's famed reaction: *"General, we expected everything from you except lessons in geometry."*

§ 2. Gaetano Fazzari's Critical Assessment (Palermo, 1901)
-----------------------------------------------------------

On the centenary of Mascheroni's death, the Sicilian mathematician and historian :person:`Gaetano Fazzari <fazzari>` produced the definitive critical edition (:cite:`fazzari-1901`). In his scholarly preface, Fazzari highlighted a crucial methodological bridge between eighteenth-century Italian constructive geometry and twentieth-century algorithmic geometry:

* While Mascheroni's proofs rely heavily on classical theorems from :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s *Elements* (:cite:`euclid-heath`) that may occasionally seem protracted to modern readers, his **constructions themselves are remarkably concise and frequently simpler than traditional ruler-and-compass methods**.
* Fazzari explicitly invoked :person:`Émile Lemoine <lemoine>`'s *Géométrographie* (:cite:`lemoine-1902`), pointing out that quantitative measures of operational simplicity :math:`S` and preparatory exactitude :math:`E` formally prove Mascheroni's compass-only solutions to be superior in manual execution to multi-instrument constructions.

§ 3. Architecture of the Twelve Books
-------------------------------------

*La Geometria del Compasso* is organized into twelve books comprising 213 propositions:

1. **Book I: Preliminary Principles** (§§ 1–11): Virtual straight lines defined by pairs of points :math:`(A, B)` and circular intersections.
2. **Book II: Division of the Circumference** (§§ 12–63): Circle quadrisection, golden ratio, inscribed pentagon, and decagon.
3. **Book III: Collinear Multiplication & Division** (§§ 64–75): Linear doubling, bisection of un-drawn segments (Solution V), and repeated subdivision.
4. **Book IV: Perpendiculars and Parallels** (§§ 76–85): Erecting perpendiculars and constructing parallel lines via rhombuses and parallelograms.
5. **Book V: Proportional Distances** (§§ 86–101): Third and fourth proportional via concentric circles, segment ratios.
6. **Book VI: Square Roots & Mean Proportionals** (§§ 102–109): Extraction of quadratic roots and mean proportional distances.
7. **Book VII: Intersections of Lines and Circles** (§§ 110–112): Intersection of a virtual line with a circle, intersection of two virtual lines.
8. **Book VIII: Angles and Trigonometric Lines** (§§ 113–126): Construction, multiplication, and bisection of angles.
9. **Book IX: Similar Figures & Regular Polygons** (§§ 127–142): Inscribing and circumscribing regular figures.
10. **Book X: Determination of Centers** (§§ 143–155): Finding the center of a given circle (Napoleon's Problem) and circumscribing triangles.
11. **Book XI: Miscellaneous Problems** (§§ 156–188): Advanced synthetic applications.
12. **Book XII: Approximation Problems** (§§ 189–213): Practical approximations for non-constructible configurations.
