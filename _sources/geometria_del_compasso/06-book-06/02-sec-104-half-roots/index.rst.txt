=======================================
§§ 104–107: Half-Roots ½√1 through ½√25
=======================================

.. folio:: 0078
   :page: 53

**104. Problem.** To find (Fig. 45) easily the halves of the roots of the whole numbers from one to twenty-five.

*Solution.* Taking the radius :math:`AB` equal to one, with center :math:`A` describe the circle :math:`BDd`, and along its circumference make :math:`AB = BC = CD = DE = Ed`.
With center :math:`B` and radius :math:`BD`, describe an arc passing through the points :math:`a, N, D, d, n, \alpha`. With the same radius and center :math:`E`, describe an arc passing through the points :math:`a, M, C, m, \alpha`. With radius :math:`Aa` and center :math:`B`, describe an arc passing through the points :math:`M, F, Q, q, m`.

   :alt: Archival Fig. 45 (folio 0078, p. 53): Compact compass lattice producing all 25 half-roots ½√1–½√25

.. folio:: 0079
   :page: 54

With the same radius and center :math:`E`, describe an arc passing through the points :math:`N, F, P, p, n`.

With radius :math:`AB` and center :math:`B`, describe an arc passing through :math:`P` and :math:`p`.

With the same radius and center :math:`E`, describe an arc passing through :math:`Q` and :math:`q`.

With the same radius and center :math:`P`, describe an arc passing through :math:`R` and cutting the circumference at :math:`S`; with center :math:`p`, mark another arc cutting the first at :math:`R` and the circumference at :math:`s`.

With the same radius and centers :math:`Q, q`, describe two arcs intersecting at :math:`T` and cutting the circumference at :math:`O` and :math:`o`.

With the same radius and center :math:`\alpha`, cut the circumference with an arc at :math:`g`. With center :math:`R`, cut the circumference with an arc at :math:`L` and :math:`l`. With centers :math:`O` and :math:`o`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`H`. With centers :math:`H` and :math:`T`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`V` and :math:`v`. We shall have

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   RA &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1} & HF &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{13} \\
   RQ &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2} & EO &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{14} \\
   RD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3} & Ll &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{15} \\
   RP &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4} & BE &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{16} \\
   RF &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5} & Ha &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{17} \\
   AM &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6} & HN &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{18} \\
   Qq &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7} & HD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{19} \\
   Aa &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8} & ag &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{20} \\
   BR &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{9} & dV &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{21} \\
   BL &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{10} & HS &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{22} \\
   pS &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{11} & Mm &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{23} \\
   BD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{12} & Mn &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{24} \\
   HE &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{25}.
   \end{aligned}

*Demonstration.* If we compare the points :math:`P, B, p, R, E` of this figure with the points :math:`A, p, B, P, Q` of Fig. 3 by means of the equation of § 18, :math:`(AQ)^2 = (Ap)^2 + pQ \cdot PQ`, we shall obtain the equation for this figure :math:`(PE)^2 = (PB)^2 + BE \cdot RE`;
that is, :math:`(Aa)^2 = (AB)^2 + 2AB \cdot RE`; or (§ 27) :math:`2 = 1 + 2RE`. Hence :math:`RE = \frac{1}{2}AE`, and since :math:`R` lies on the same straight line :math:`BAE` (§ 13), we shall also have :math:`RA = \frac{1}{2}AE = \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1}`.

Since point :math:`T` is at the midpoint of :math:`AB` for the same

.. folio:: 0080
   :page: 55

reason
by which point :math:`R` was proved to be at the midpoint of :math:`AE`, it will be :math:`AT = RE`; hence comparing the points :math:`Q, T, q, E, R` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`A, q, B, Q, P` of Fig. 3, point :math:`A` of Fig. 45 will be point :math:`p` of Fig. 3.
Therefore from the equation of § 16, :math:`(AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ`, we shall obtain for this Fig. 45 the equation :math:`(QE)^2 = (RQ)^2 + (RE)^2 + AR \cdot RE`; that is, substituting the numerical values, :math:`1 = (RQ)^2 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}`. Hence :math:`(RQ)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2`; :math:`RQ = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}`.

Comparing the points :math:`D, A, d, E` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`P, A, p, B` of Fig. 3, it will remain proved from § 14 that the two :math:`AE, Dd` mutually bisect each other. But :math:`AE` is bisected at :math:`R`; therefore :math:`Dd` is also. But :math:`Dd = BD = \sqrt{3}` (§ 2). Therefore :math:`RD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}`.

Note that :math:`DRd` is also perpendicular to :math:`AE` (§ 14).

Next we have :math:`RP = 1`. Therefore :math:`RP = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4}`.

Since the angle :math:`FAR` is right (§ 27), we have :math:`(RF)^2 = (FA)^2 + (AR)^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}`. Therefore :math:`RF = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}`.

Since the base :math:`BAE` of the triangle :math:`BME` is bisected by the straight line :math:`AM`, we shall have by § 26, :math:`(BM)^2 + (EM)^2 = 2(AB)^2 + 2(AM)^2`; that is, :math:`(Aa)^2 + (BD)^2 = 2(AB)^2 + 2(AM)^2`; that is, :math:`2 + 3 = 2 + 2(AM)^2`. Hence :math:`6 = 4(AM)^2`; :math:`\sqrt{6} = 2AM`; :math:`AM = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6}`.

Having compared above the points :math:`Q, T, q, E, R, A` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`A, q, B, Q, P, p` of Fig. 3, it will follow from § 13 that in this Fig. 45 :math:`AQ = Aq = QR = Rq`. For the same reasons, the four :math:`AP, Ap, PT, Tp` will turn out equal to one another and to these four.
Therefore, since the two isosceles triangles :math:`PTA, QAR` have all their sides equal to each other, the angle :math:`PAT = QRA` (I.8), and since :math:`TAR` is a straight line, :math:`PA` will be parallel to :math:`QR` (I.29). Hence :math:`PQ` is also equal and parallel to :math:`AR` (I.33).

But :math:`Qq` is perpendicular to :math:`AR` (§ 14). Therefore also to :math:`PQ` (I.27).
Next, in the two triangles :math:`PAT, RAq`, the two angles :math:`PAT, RAq` are equal (I.8), and :math:`TAR` is a straight line. Therefore, since the two angles :math:`PAT, PAR` are equal to two right angles (I.13),

.. folio:: 0081
   :page: 56

so also will be the two :math:`PAR, RAq`.
Hence :math:`PAq` will also be a straight line (I.14). Therefore :math:`(Pq)^2 = (2RQ)^2 = (PQ)^2 + (Qq)^2` (I.47); that is, :math:`2 = \frac{1}{4} + (Qq)^2`; hence :math:`\frac{7}{4} = (Qq)^2`, and :math:`Qq = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7}`.

Next we have :math:`(Aa)^2 = 2` (§ 27) :math:`= \frac{8}{4}`. Hence :math:`Aa = \frac{\sqrt{8}}{\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8}`.

Also let :math:`BR = \frac{3}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{9}`.

If we compare the points :math:`B, L, R, l, A` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`Q, A, p, B, P` of Fig. 3, from the equation :math:`(AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ` of § 16 we shall obtain for this Fig. 45 the equation :math:`(BL)^2 = (LA)^2 + (AB)^2 + AR \cdot AB`; that is, :math:`(BL)^2 = 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{10}{4}`. Hence :math:`BL = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{10}`.

The two triangles :math:`PSA, PBA` have their sides respectively equal. Therefore the angle :math:`SPA = PAB` (I.8). Hence :math:`PS` and :math:`BA` are parallel (I.28). But :math:`Pp` cuts :math:`BR` at right angles (§ 14). Therefore it will also be perpendicular to :math:`PS` (I.27).
Then in the same manner as :math:`(Qq)^2 = \frac{7}{4}` was proved, it will also be proved that :math:`(Pp)^2 = \frac{7}{4}`. Hence since :math:`(pS)^2 = (Pp)^2 + (PS)^2` (I.47), we shall have :math:`(pS)^2 = \frac{7}{4} + 1 = \frac{11}{4}`; hence :math:`pS = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{11}`.

Next we have :math:`(BD)^2 = 3` (§ 2) :math:`= \frac{12}{4}`. Hence :math:`BD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{12}`.

We also have :math:`(HF)^2 = (HA)^2 + (AF)^2`; and since :math:`QO` is proved parallel to :math:`BE` in the same manner as was proved for :math:`PS`, the points :math:`O, P, Q, S` will lie on the same straight line, and :math:`PO = QO - PQ = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} = PQ`.
Therefore, since :math:`OP` is equal and parallel both to :math:`TA` and to :math:`TB`, :math:`OT, PA` will also be equal and parallel, and the two :math:`OB, PT` to each other (I.33). But it was proved above that :math:`PT = PA`. Therefore it will also be equal to these, :math:`= OT = OB`.
For the same reason, on the other side :math:`oT, oB` will be equal to :math:`pA = PA`. Now if we compare the points :math:`O, o, A, T, B, H` with the points :math:`A, B, Q, P, p, q` of Fig. 3, it will follow from § 14 that :math:`HB = AT = \frac{1}{2}`. Therefore :math:`(HA)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 = \frac{9}{4}`; hence :math:`(HF)^2 = \frac{9}{4} + 1 = \frac{13}{4}` and :math:`HF = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{13}`.

If we compare the points :math:`E, O, H, o, A` with the points :math:`Q, A, p, B, P` of Fig. 3, from the equation :math:`(AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ` of § 16 we shall obtain for this Figure :math:`(EO)^2 = (OA)^2 + (AE)^2 + HA \cdot AE = 1 + 1 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{7}{2} = \frac{14}{4}`. Hence :math:`EO = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{14}`.

Next, if we compare the points :math:`A, R, L, Q, q, l` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`A, B, Q, P, p, q` of Fig. 3,

.. folio:: 0082
   :page: 57

having (§ 15) the equation for Fig. 3, :math:`(QM)^2 = (AQ)^2 - (AM)^2`,
and multiplying by 4, :math:`4(QM)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - 4(AM)^2`, or :math:`(Qq)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - (AB)^2`, we shall obtain for this Fig. 45, :math:`(Ll)^2 = 4(AL)^2 - (AR)^2 = 4 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{15}{4}`; hence :math:`Ll = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{15}`.

Next we have :math:`BE = 2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{16}`.

By the right angle :math:`aAH` (§ 27), we have :math:`(Ha)^2 = (HA)^2 + (Aa)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 + 2 = \frac{17}{4}`; hence :math:`Ha = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{17}`.

Since it is proved in the same way that :math:`(AN)^2 = \frac{3}{2}`, as was proved for :math:`(AM)^2`, and likewise :math:`(An)^2 = \frac{3}{2}`; because :math:`NR` bisects the base :math:`AE` of the triangle :math:`ANE`, we shall have (§ 26) :math:`(AN)^2 + (NE)^2 = 2(AR)^2 + 2(RN)^2`; that is, :math:`\frac{3}{2} + 2 = \frac{2}{4} + 2(RN)^2`; and reducing we find :math:`(RN)^2 = \frac{3}{2} = (AN)^2`. Likewise we find :math:`(Rn)^2 = \frac{3}{2}`.
Now if we compare the points :math:`H, N, R, n, A` of this Fig. 45 with the points :math:`Q, A, p, B, P` of Fig. 3, from the equation :math:`(AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ` of § 16 we shall obtain for Fig. 45 the equation :math:`(HN)^2 = (AN)^2 + (AH)^2 + AR \cdot AH`, that is, :math:`(HN)^2 = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{18}{4}`. Hence :math:`HN = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{18}`.

Since :math:`DR` is perpendicular to :math:`AE`, that is, to :math:`HR`, we shall have :math:`(HD)^2 = (HR)^2 + (RD)^2` (I.47) :math:`= 4 + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{19}{4}`; hence :math:`HD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{19}`.

Since the base :math:`aA\alpha` of the triangle :math:`ag\alpha` is bisected by the straight line :math:`gA`, we shall have (§ 26) :math:`(ag)^2 + (\alpha g)^2 = 2(Aa)^2 + 2(Ag)^2`; that is, :math:`(ag)^2 + 1 = 4 + 2`; and :math:`(ag)^2 = 5 = \frac{20}{4}`; hence :math:`ag = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{20}`.

Since the two triangles :math:`HTV, AED` have their sides equal to each other, the angle :math:`VTH = DEA` (I.8), and since the points :math:`H, T, A, E` lie on the same straight line, :math:`VT` will be parallel to its equal :math:`DE` (I.29), and hence :math:`VD` is also parallel and equal to :math:`TE` (I.33).
But :math:`Dd` is perpendicular to :math:`AE`, that is, to :math:`TE`; therefore also to :math:`VD` (I.27). Hence :math:`(dV)^2 = (VD)^2 + (Dd)^2 = (TE)^2 + (BD)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 + 3` (§ 2) :math:`= \frac{9}{4} + \frac{12}{4} = \frac{21}{4}`; hence :math:`dV = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{21}`.

Having proved :math:`PS` equal and parallel to :math:`AE`, and :math:`ps` for the same reason, :math:`SE` will also be equal and parallel to :math:`AP` (I.33), and likewise :math:`sE` to :math:`Ap`. By the equality and parallelism of the three :math:`PS, TR, ps`, the equality of :math:`RS, Rs` to :math:`PT, pT`, both proved equal to :math:`AP = RQ`, will be proved in the same way.
Comparing now the points :math:`H, S, E, s, R` of this Figure with the points :math:`Q, A, p, B, P` of Fig. 3,

.. folio:: 0083
   :page: 58

from the equation :math:`(AQ)^2 = (Ap)^2 + pQ \cdot PQ` (§ 18) we shall obtain for this Fig. 45, :math:`(HS)^2 = (SE)^2 + EH \cdot RH = (RQ)^2 + EH \cdot RH = \frac{2}{4} + \frac{5}{2} \cdot 2 = \frac{22}{4}`. Hence :math:`HS = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{22}`.

Since the base :math:`AE` of the triangle :math:`AME` is bisected by :math:`MR`, we shall have (§ 26) :math:`(AM)^2 + (EM)^2 = 2(RA)^2 + 2(RM)^2`; that is, :math:`\frac{6}{4} + 3 = \frac{2}{4} + 2(RM)^2`. Hence reducing we obtain :math:`(RM)^2 = 2 = (BM)^2 = (Bm)^2`; of which value :math:`(Rm)^2` is proved to be in the same way. Hence :math:`BM = MR = Rm = mB`.
Now if we compare the four points :math:`B, M, R, m` of this Figure with the four points :math:`A, Q, B, q` of Fig. 3, since from § 15 we have :math:`(QM)^2 = (AQ)^2 - (AM)^2`, and hence :math:`4(QM)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - 4(AM)^2`, that is, :math:`(Qq)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - (AB)^2`, we shall have for this Fig. 45, :math:`(Mm)^2 = 4(BM)^2 - (BR)^2 = 8 - (\frac{3}{2})^2 = \frac{32}{4} - \frac{9}{4} = \frac{23}{4}`; hence :math:`Mm = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{23}`.

Since the triangles :math:`BME, BnE` have their sides equal to each other, and both have the common base :math:`BE` bisected by :math:`AM, An`, from the equation of § 26 the same value will result for the two :math:`AM, An`. Therefore, since the triangles :math:`BAM, EAn` have their sides equal to each other, the angle :math:`BAM = EAn` (I.8).
Hence, because of the straight line :math:`BAE`, since the sum of the two angles :math:`MAB, MAE` equals two right angles (I.13), substituting for the angle :math:`MAB` its equal :math:`EAn`, the sum of the two angles :math:`MAE, EAn` will also equal two right angles.
Therefore the two :math:`MA, An` will form a single straight line (I.14). Thus :math:`(Mn)^2 = 4(AM)^2 = \frac{24}{4}`. Hence :math:`Mn = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{24}`.

Finally :math:`HE = \frac{5}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{25}`.

**105.** Since :math:`\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{\frac{n}{4}}`—for example, :math:`\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7} = \sqrt{\frac{7}{4}}`, etc.—
we shall easily have from this Problem the roots of all fourths from one fourth up to twenty-five fourths; which will be of use in constructing similar Figures, as we shall see.

**106.** If we had taken the radius :math:`AB = 2`, we would have obtained all these distances doubled in value;
hence we would have had the whole-number roots of the numbers from one to twenty-five. However, we could easily double any of those half-roots that we wished, in order to obtain the whole root (§ 64).

**107.** The ease of this construction, which is executed with only the three compasses of the three openings
already noted (§ 35)—the first :math:`= \sqrt{1}`, the third :math:`= \sqrt{2}`, the second :math:`= \sqrt{3}`—with which the circle was already divided into 24 equal parts, and now 25
