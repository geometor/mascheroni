Commentary & Notes: Mohr, Mascheroni, Bonaparte & Fazzari¶

Historical Lineage, Priority, and Reception of La Geometria del Compasso

1. Georg Mohr (1672) and the Priority of Euclides Danicus¶

In 1672, the Danish mathematician Georg Mohr published Euclides Danicus (mohr-1672) in Dutch (Euclides Danicus, Bestaende udi Too Deele) at Amsterdam. In Part I of that work, Mohr systematically demonstrated that all 53 problems of Books I–VI of Euclid’s Elements can be constructed with the compass alone.

Because Mohr’s booklet had an obscure Dutch/Danish printing and was cataloged among practical perspective works, it remained entirely unknown to eighteenth-century European scholars, including Mascheroni, Lagrange, Laplace, and Gauss. Only in 1928 did Johannes Hjelmslev examine a copy found by a student in a Copenhagen antiquarian bookshop, establishing Mohr’s complete 125-year priority. The theorem is therefore rightfully designated today as the Mohr–Mascheroni Theorem.

2. Mascheroni’s Independent Creation and Methodological Superiority¶

While Mohr preceded Mascheroni chronologically, Lorenzo Mascheroni’s La Geometria del Compasso (mascheroni-1797) stands as an independent, vastly more comprehensive constructive masterpiece:

  • Mascheroni created a systematic synthetic calculus across twelve books, providing multiple constructive pathways for each problem (e.g., five distinct solutions for bisecting a segment in § 66).

  • Mascheroni introduced the explicit representation of straight lines by point-pairs \((A, B)\), formulating modern projective and metric coordinates without continuous curves.

  • Through Antoine-Michel Carette’s 1798 French translation (carette-1798), Mascheroni directly influenced nineteenth-century synthetic geometry, leading to Jakob Steiner’s 1833 dual fixed-circle straightedge geometry (steiner-1833) and August Adler’s 1890 inversion theory (adler-1890).

3. Gaetano Fazzari and Émile Lemoine’s Géométrographie¶

In his 1901 centenary Palermo edition (fazzari-1901), Gaetano Fazzari brought Mascheroni into direct dialogue with the quantitative science of Émile Lemoine’s Géométrographie (lemoine-1902).

Fazzari observed that critics who had labeled compass-only geometry as a mere “mathematical curiosity” overlooked its genuine operational economy: by dispensing with straightedge alignment operations (\(R_1, R_2\)), many Mascheroni constructions actually achieve lower simplicity \(S\) and superior drafting exactitude \(E\) than standard ruler-and-compass procedures taught in Euclidean schools.
