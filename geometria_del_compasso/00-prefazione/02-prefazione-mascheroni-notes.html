Notes: Mascheroni’s 1797 Preface¶

Instrumental, Chemical, and Philological Commentary

Pneumatic Chemistry Allusion¶

Mascheroni’s phrase:

« …a guisa di chi ha separata l’acqua in due arie, e qualche aria pure stimata semplice, in due altre sostanze? »

directly refers to the Chemical Revolution led by Antoine Lavoisier in the 1780s, who decomposed water into hydrogen (air inflammable) and oxygen (air vital). Mascheroni, writing in Pavia in 1797, draws an explicit parallel between the decomposition of chemical compounds into elementary substances and the decomposition of Euclidean constructions into pure compass operations.

Astronomical Instrument Makers¶

  1. George Graham (1673–1751): English clockmaker, geophysicist, and Fellow of the Royal Society. Graham constructed the 8-foot iron mural quadrant for Edmond Halley at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich (1725). His method relied on continuous chord bisection and stepping off calculated chord lengths with dividers.

  2. John Bird (1709–1776): Succeeded Graham as the foremost instrument maker in London. Bird’s 1767 treatise The Method of Dividing Astronomical Instruments documented the technique of continuous bisection ($90^\circ = 60^\circ + 30^\circ$, bisected into $15^\circ$, etc.), using beam compasses equipped with micrometer screws.

  3. Jesse Ramsden (1735–1800): Invented the mechanical dividing engine (1775, Board of Longitude award), allowing circles to be graduated automatically by an endless screw gear without manual chord stepping.

Lemoine’s Exactitude Metric¶

In 1890, Émile Lemoine formalized Mascheroni’s intuitive argument into the metric of Exactitude ($E = m_1 + m_2$): * Compass placement ($C_1$) involves setting a sharp steel point precisely into a center pricking, with error bounded by microscope resolution. * Straightedge placement ($L_1$) requires aligning a straight bar simultaneously across two distant points while maintaining zero lateral clearance, doubling positional uncertainty.
