§§ 193–214: Fundamental Chord Equations & Sexagesimal Degrees¶

BOOK TWELVE

PROBLEMS BY APPROXIMATION

193. All problems higher than the second degree cannot be solved geometrically with straightedge and compass alone, but require intersections of conic curves or of higher degrees; therefore neither can they be solved exactly by the Geometry of the Compass alone. Instruments for describing the cycloid, the conchoid, the cissoid, the tractrix, and other such curves, which serve for the resolution of those problems, though invented with great ingenuity and proving elegant and expeditious in practice, nevertheless, when it is not a question of having the entire course of that curve for which they are intended, but only of obtaining a point by its intersection with other lines,

f. 0146 · p. 121

certainly leave in practice such doubt of small errors, sometimes not easily calculable, that in very many cases one will prefer to the theoretical exactness of those methods a sufficiently close practical approximation from a construction made with compass and straightedge. In these cases, I say, most of the time a solution obtained with the compass alone will still be preferable. The examples will show this to whoever wishes to compare our solutions with those already known.

194. Since there is as yet no general method for obtaining these approximations with Elementary Geometry, it should not be expected that I propose one for my Geometry of the Compass either. I do not call a Geometrical method of approximation that of obtaining a value approximately with the aid of one of those scales that are called geometrical, since an arithmetical calculation must precede its use; the method itself should rather be called arithmetical. Suppose, for example, that one desires the cube root of the number 2; the extraction that one must make arithmetically of this root in order then to take the decimal parts or fractions of another kind upon a geometrical scale with the compass, for the purpose of duplicating some cube, makes the expedient belong to Arithmetic rather than to Geometry.

195. I have not been able to devise hitherto any other means of obtaining approximately the solution of many useful problems higher than the second degree, beyond that of finding various kinds, and as it were classes, of constructions of elementary figures; hence of subjecting to calculation the greatest number possible of the nearly innumerable particular cases that result from them; choosing among these those that best serve the purpose, and using them to solve the problem.

196. Of these kinds, and as it were classes of constructions, I have examined many, and I keep apart some researches on the same. The simplest class, and that of which we shall make almost exclusive use in this last Book of our Geometry, is founded upon the three memorable points \(a\), \(b\), and \(e\) (Figs. 9, 11, and 12) (§ 59), which have already served us so much in the preceding books, and upon which we now proceed to set forth the twelve equations promised by us (§ 59).

197. Let (Fig. 12) the point \(Z\) be considered as any

f. 0147 · p. 122

point taken upon the quadrant \(BF\) in Fig. 12 constructed as in Book II, and in the other quadrant \(Bf\) let \(z\) be the point such that \(Bz = BZ\). We shall have (§§ 20 and 21)

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} (A) \quad (aZ)^2 &= (aB)^2 - Zz \cdot Aa, \\ (B) \quad (bZ)^2 &= (bB)^2 + Zz \cdot Ab, \\ (E) \quad (eZ)^2 &= (eB)^2 - Zz \cdot Ae. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

198. If we wish the equations for the distances of the three points \(a\), \(b\), and \(e\) from point \(z\), we shall only have to change the signs in the second member of the preceding equations, and we shall have (§§ 20 and 21)

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} (A') \quad (az)^2 &= (aB)^2 + zZ \cdot Aa, \\ (B') \quad (bz)^2 &= (bB)^2 - zZ \cdot Ab, \\ (E') \quad (ez)^2 &= (eB)^2 + zZ \cdot Ae. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

199. Since \(Zz\), the chord of the double arc of \(BZ\), is \(= 2\sin BZ\), and under the supposition \(AB = 1\), which we shall always retain, \((aB)^2 = (BD)^2 = 3\) (§ 2); \((Aa)^2 = 2\) (§ 27); \((Bb)^2 = \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}\) (§ 182); \((Ae) = 2 - \sqrt{2}\) (§ 38), hence \((eB)^2 = (AB)^2 + (Ae)^2 = 3 - \sqrt{2}\); \(Ab = \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{5} - 1)\) (§ 181); if we put \(aZ = a\), we shall have \((A') \dots a^2 = 3 - 2\sin A \cdot \sqrt{2}\); under this equation we also include the case where the arc \(A\) is negative, namely \(Bz\), in which the sign prefixed to \(2\sin A \cdot \sqrt{2}\) is changed to \(+\).

200. The value of \(a\) can always be that of some chord of the circle \(BDd\), except in the case where the distance \(az\) expressed by \(a\) exceeds the value 2. To calculate the value of \(a\) more easily in these cases, since \(\sqrt{2} = BF = 2\sin 45^\circ\), and \(2\sin A \sin 45^\circ = \cos(A - 45^\circ) - \cos(A + 45^\circ) = \cos(45^\circ - A) - \sin(45^\circ - A)\) (§ 159), we shall have

\[(*) \quad a^2 = 3 - 2\cos(45^\circ - A) + 2\sin(45^\circ - A).\]

201. In the other cases, in which \(a\) is less than 2, calling \(A'\) that arc of which \(a\) is chord, we shall have \(\frac{a^2}{4} = \sin^2 \frac{1}{2} A' = \frac{1 - \cos A'}{2}\) (§ 156) \(= \frac{3}{4} - \sin A \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{3}{4} - \sin A \cdot \cos 45^\circ\); whence reducing \(\cos A' = 2\sin A \cdot \cos 45^\circ - \frac{1}{2}\); hence again (§ 157)

\[\cos A' = \sin(A + 45^\circ) + \sin(A - 45^\circ) - \sin 30^\circ,\]

or

\[(1) \quad \cos A' = \cos(45^\circ - A) - \sin(45^\circ - A) - \sin 30^\circ.\]
f. 0148 · p. 123

202. If one wishes to make this equation (1) serve for dividing the circumference into those parts that cannot be obtained with precision, we shall introduce in place of \(A\) an exact arc, for instance the twentieth part (Fig. 12) of the circumference \(= 18^\circ\), supposing \(BZ = 18^\circ\), and we shall have \(\cos A' = \cos 27^\circ - \sin 27^\circ - \sin 30^\circ\).

Now

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \sin 27^\circ &= 0.4539905 \\ \sin 30^\circ &= 0.5 \\ \hline &\phantom{=} 0.9539905 \\ \cos 27^\circ &= 0.8910065 \\ \hline \text{Hence} \quad \cos A' &= -0.0629840 \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Then one finds in the tables \(0.0629840 = \sin 3^\circ 36' \frac{1935}{2903}\). Now since \(\frac{1935}{2903}\) is so close to the value \(\frac{2}{3}\) that there is not an error of even a whole unit in the last digit of the number 1935, one may take \(0.0629840\) for the sine of \(3^\circ 36' 40''\) without being able to decide by common tables whether there is even the error of a third-minute. Therefore the arc \(A'\), which has this negative cosine, will be \(= 93^\circ 36' 40''\), and of this arc the distance \(aZ\) will be chord, setting \(BZ = 18^\circ\). Applying then the distance \(aZ\) taken on the compass as chord to the circumference, such an arc \(= 93^\circ 36' 40''\) will be determined, very approximately.

203. To investigate the error, which escapes the numbers of common tables, consider that since the sine of \(18^\circ\) is equal to half the chord of the tenth part of the circumference, namely \(= \frac{1}{2} Ab = \frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{5} - 1)\), and the cosine of \(45^\circ = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}\), the equation \(\cos A' = 2\sin A \cos 45^\circ - \frac{1}{2}\) of § 201 gives \(\cos A' = \frac{1}{4}\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{5} - 1) - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{10} - \sqrt{2}) - \frac{1}{2} = 0.0629839824\). Having then calculated to more digits the sine of \(3^\circ 36' 40''\) as well, we find it \(= 0.062984061154\). Keeping only eight decimal digits, we find \(\sin 3^\circ 36' 39'' = 0.06297921\). The difference for \(1''\), or for \(60'''\), is \(485\). If then \(485\) gives \(60'''\), \(8\) will give \(\frac{96'''}{97}\); thus it will not give a whole third-minute. Therefore the arc of which the distance \(aZ\) is chord is not greater than \(93^\circ 36' 40''\) by a whole third-minute.

204. All this being granted, here is the use that could be made of this value for the ancient division of the circle. Passing over (Fig. 12) that it could serve to divide a prime minute into three

f. 0149 · p. 124

in those circles where prime minutes were marked, since one would find the \(40''\), that is \(\frac{2'}{3}\), between one prime minute and the next following, and this without the error of a third-minute; it can also serve to find the third part of a degree. For since arc \(cBN = 90^\circ\) and \(Np = 3^\circ\) (§§ 31, 43), if taking \(BZ = Kp\), in which case arc \(BZ\) comes out \(= 18^\circ\) (§ 32), with radius \(aZ\) and center \(c\) an arc be described, it will cut the circumference between \(p\) and \(P\) at a point distant from \(p\) by \(36' 40''\) with the small error calculated. Let this point be called \(y\), and let us triple, for example, the arc \(Ny = 3^\circ 36' 40''\). We shall have an arc \(= 10^\circ 50'\) without the error of three third-minutes, and if this triplication has been made from \(N\) toward \(G\), the last division will fall between \(\pi\) and \(\varphi\). Let \(\eta\) be called the point where it falls, so that \(N\eta = 10^\circ 50'\). Let \(\mu\) then be called the point that is at the midpoint of the arc \(\pi\varphi\) obtained by § 58. The arc \(\mu\eta\) will be \(= 20'\) without the error of three third-minutes, that is, one will have obtained a third of an ancient degree with so much precision that I do not know whether for practice a greater could be desired.

205. We have drawn this example from the list of all calculated values of \(\cos A'\), introducing into equation (1) in place of \(A\) successively \(90^\circ, 88^\circ 30', 87^\circ\), and so on down to \(0^\circ\), then \(-1^\circ 30', -3^\circ\), etc. down to \(-19^\circ 30'\) inclusively; beyond which case equation (1) no longer holds, its values becoming greater than 1.

206. We shall now proceed to find the remaining equations. Every distance from point \(b\) to the points of the circumference, turning out less than the diameter 2, can be the chord of an arc. Let this arc, of which \(bZ\) is chord, be called \(B'\); we shall have \(\frac{1}{2} bZ = \sin \frac{1}{2} B'\), and calling \(B\) the arc \(BZ\), we shall have \(Zz = 2\sin B\); hence from equation \((B)\) of § 197, divided by 4, will result \(\sin^2 \frac{1}{2} B' = \frac{1 - \cos B'}{2}\) (§ 156) \(= \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{8} + \sin B \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{4}\). Therefore \(\cos B' = 1 - \left(\frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{4}\right) - \sin B \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} = 1 - 2\sin^2 36^\circ - 2\sin B \sin 18^\circ = \cos 72^\circ - \cos(B - 18^\circ) + \cos(B + 18^\circ)\) (§§ 156, 159). We shall therefore have

\[(2) \quad \cos B' = \sin 18^\circ + \cos(B + 18^\circ) - \cos(B - 18^\circ).\]

207. Likewise, if \(E'\) be called the arc of which \(eZ\) is chord, and \(E\) the arc \(BZ\), we shall have from equation \((E)\) (§ 197)

f. 0150 · p. 125

\(\sin^2 \frac{1}{2} E' = \frac{1 - \cos E'}{2} = \frac{3 - \sqrt{2}}{4} - \sin E \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{2}\). Hence it follows

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \cos E' &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{2} + 2\sin E \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{2} \\ &= \sin 45^\circ - \sin 30^\circ + 2\sin E \sin 22^\circ 30'. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Therefore finally

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} (3) \quad \cos E' = \sin 45^\circ - \sin 30^\circ &+ \cos(E - 22^\circ 30') \\ &- \cos(E + 22^\circ 30'). \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

208. By means of these three equations, with the aid of the tables of natural sines and cosines, given an arc \(A\), \(B\), and \(E\), by simple additions or subtractions we shall obtain the cosines, and hence the arcs \(A'\), \(B'\), and \(E'\), of which the distances \(aZ\), \(bZ\), \(eZ\) are chords, which distances themselves will be known by doubling the sine of half the arcs \(A'\), \(B'\), and \(E'\).

209. Conversely, if the distance \(aZ\) be equal to a chord of a known arc \(A'\), and it be sought of what arc \(Zz\) will become chord, or of how many degrees the arc \(BZ\), which is its half and is \(= A\), will turn out; from the equation (§ 199) \(a^2 = 3 - 2\sin A \cdot \sqrt{2}\), we shall obtain \(\sin A = \frac{3 - a^2}{2\sqrt{2}}\), and substituting the value of \(a^2 = 4\sin^2 \frac{1}{2} A' = 2 - 2\cos A'\) (§ 156), we shall have \(\sin A = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \cos A' \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} = \sin 30^\circ \sin 45^\circ + \cos A' \sin 45^\circ\), and hence (§§ 157, 159) since \(\sin(45^\circ + A') = \cos(45^\circ - A')\)

\[(4) \quad \sin A = \frac{1}{2} [\sin 45^\circ + \sin(45^\circ - A') + \cos(45^\circ - A')].\]

210. Likewise, if the arc \(B'\) be known, of which the distance \(bZ\) is chord, and the arc \(B = BZ\) be sought by means of \(Zz\), which is chord of \(2B\); multiplying equation \((B)\) (§ 197) by \(\sqrt{5} + 1\), since \((bB)^2 = \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}\) and \(Ab = \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{5} - 1)\), we shall have \((bZ)^2 (\sqrt{5} + 1) = 2\sqrt{5} + 4\sin B\), and substituting the value of \((bZ)^2 = 4\sin^2 \frac{1}{2} B' = 2 - 2\cos B'\) (§ 156), we shall have after making the reductions \(\sin B = \frac{1}{2} - 2\cos B' \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{4}\right)\). Now since \(Bb\) is the side of the pentagon \(= \sqrt{\frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}}\), chord of \(72^\circ\), we shall have \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2}} = \sin 36^\circ\). Hence \(\frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{5 - \sqrt{5}}{2} = \sin^2 36^\circ\); hence \(1 - \sin^2 36^\circ = \cos^2 36^\circ = \sin^2 54^\circ\)

f. 0151 · p. 126

\(= \frac{6 + 2\sqrt{5}}{16} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{4}\right)^2\); hence it will result \(\sin B = \frac{1}{2} - 2\cos B' \sin 54^\circ\), and finally (§ 157)

\[(5) \quad \sin B = \sin 30^\circ - \sin(54^\circ + B') - \sin(54^\circ - B').\]

211. In the same manner, if the arc \(E'\) be known, of which the distance \(eZ\) is chord, and the degree of the arc \(BZ = E\) be sought, equal to half the arc of which \(Zz = 2\sin E\) is chord, substituting in equation \((E)\) (§ 197) the value of \((eZ)^2 = 2 - 2\cos E'\), and the other values of \((eB)^2 = 3 - \sqrt{2}\); \(Ae = \sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}} = 2\sin 22^\circ 30'\) (§§ 199, 38), we shall have \(2\sin E \cdot \sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}} = 2\cos E' + 1 - \sqrt{2}\), and multiplying by \(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}}\), there will result

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} 4\sin E &= 2\cos E' \cdot \sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} - (\sqrt{2} - 1)\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} \\ &= 2\cos E' \cdot \sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} - (2 - \sqrt{2})\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} \\ &= 2\cos E' \cdot \sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} - \sqrt{2}\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

and dividing by 4, and considering that \(\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} = 2\sin 67^\circ 30'\) (§ 37), \(\sqrt{2} = 2\sin 45^\circ\), we shall have (§§ 157, 158, 159) \(\sin E = 2\cos E' \sin 67^\circ 30' \sin 45^\circ - \sin 22^\circ 30' \sin 45^\circ\); or \(\sin E = \cos E' [\cos(67^\circ 30' - 45^\circ) - \cos(67^\circ 30' + 45^\circ)] - \sin 22^\circ 30' \cdot \sin 45^\circ\), or \(\sin E = \cos E' \cos 22^\circ 30' + \cos E' \sin 22^\circ 30' - \sin 22^\circ 30' \sin 45^\circ\) or finally

\[\begin{split}(6) \quad \sin E = \frac{1}{2} \left\{ \begin{aligned} &\cos(E' + 22^\circ 30') \\ + &\sin(E' + 22^\circ 30') \\ + &\sin 22^\circ 30' \\ + &\cos(E' - 22^\circ 30') \\ - &\sin(E' - 22^\circ 30') \\ - &\cos 22^\circ 30' \end{aligned} \right\}\end{split}\]

212. Thus from the three equations \((A)\), \((B)\), and \((E)\) we have derived six, by means of which from given arcs we can obtain new arcs with the aid of the three points \(a\), \(b\), and \(e\) alone, and together with them their new chords.

213. Since the given arcs can be infinite, the cases of each of these equations are also infinite. But if we wish to avail ourselves only of the arcs that can be found by means of those same three points \(a\), \(b\), and \(e\), they would be 120 for each equation, if for some of them there were not

f. 0152 · p. 127

certain particular limits. Indeed, since by means of those three points the circumference can be divided into 240 equal parts (§ 57), and consequently the semicircumference into 120, there will be 60 points \(Z\) and 60 points \(z\), which taken together would determine 120 cases for each equation. But some of them will have particular limits.

214. For example, if I wished to take on the compass the chord of \(3^\circ\), that is, of the one-hundred-twentieth part of the circumference (§ 42), and placing the point of the compass at \(a\) to mark an arc, it could not cut the circumference at any point \(Z\), since this chord is less than the distance \(aF = \sqrt{2} - 1\). Therefore we cannot introduce into equation (4) in place of \(A'\) the arc of \(3^\circ\), and if one wished to do so, there would result a value greater than unity, that is, absurd for \(\sin A\). To see what is the first arc that can be introduced in place of \(A'\) among the arcs of the series \(1^\circ 30', 3^\circ, 4^\circ 30'\), etc., observe of what arc the distance \(aF\) is chord, and it is \(= \sqrt{2} - 1 = 2\sin 45^\circ - \frac{1}{2} = 0.91421356 = \cos 23^\circ 54'+\). Therefore the first arc that can be used among those found by means of the three points \(a\), \(b\), and \(e\) will be the arc of \(24^\circ\). After it, one may then use all the others in the series \(25^\circ 30', 27^\circ, 29^\circ 30'\), etc., up to \(180^\circ\), since the chords of all these can be distances from point \(a\) to some point \(Z\) or \(z\) of the circumference.
