§§ 30–35: Division into 8, 12, and 24 Parts¶

30. Prob. To divide a circumference into eight equal parts.

Sol. Remaining as in § 27 (fig. 9), let \(AB = aG = aH\) with the 1st compass, \(Aa = Gg = Hh\) with the 3rd compass; it will also be \(Aa = gh\); and the circumference will be divided into eight equal parts at the points \(B, G, F, H, E, h, f, g\).

Dem. Since \((Aa)^2 = 2\) (§ 27), we shall have \((Aa)^2 = (AG)^2 + (aG)^2\). Therefore the angle \(aGA\) will be right (I.48). Hence in the isosceles triangle \(aGA\) the other two angles \(GAa, GaA\), equal to each other (I.5), will be half-right (I.32). Therefore the angle \(GAF\), which is the same as the angle \(GAa\) (§ 28), will be half of \(BAF\). Thus also the arc \(GF = BG\). But by construction \(Gg = BF\) (III.26). Therefore subtracting \(BG\) from both sides, \(GF = Bg\). In the same way it will be demonstrated that the other arcs are equal. The circumference will thus be divided into equal parts each half of the quadrant, and hence into eight.

31. Prob. To divide the circumference into twelve equal parts.

Sol. Remaining as in § 27 (fig. 9), let \(AB\)

\(= FN = Nn = FO = Oo.\) The circumference will be divided into twelve equal parts at the points \(B, N, C, F, D, O, E, o, d, f, c, n\).

Dem. Since taking away the equal arcs \(BC, DE\) from the equal arcs \(BF, FE\), the remaining arcs \(CF, FD\) will be equal. Since \(CD\) is the sixth part of the circumference (§ 29), \(CF\) will be its half, namely the twelfth. Also \(CF = CN\) because \(FN = CD\); likewise \(CN = NB\) because \(FN = CB\). And in the same manner it will be demonstrated that all the others are twelfth parts of the circumference.

32. Prob. To divide the same circumference into twenty-four equal parts.

Sol. Remaining as above (fig. 9) (§§ 30 and 31), let \(AB = GL = LM = Gk = ki = HI = IK = Hm = ml\) with the 1st compass, and it will be done.

Dem. Since if from the equal arcs \(GF, GB\) (§ 30) we subtract the equal arcs \(CF, NB\) (§ 31), \(GC, GN\) will remain equal; and since \(CN\) is a twelfth part of the circumference (§ 31), \(GC, GN\) will be twenty-fourth parts of it. Then since \(FN = GL\), taking away \(FG\), we have \(NG = FL\). Hence \(FL\) will also be a twenty-fourth and half of \(FD\) (§ 31). In the same way the arcs \(DH, HO, FI, IC\) will be proved equal to these, as well as all the others determined above.

33. We have here used without citing them Props. 26 and 27 of Book III of Euclid, that in a circle or in equal circles equal straight lines subtend equal arcs; which we shall also do hereafter for brevity.

34. The ancients, using center \(A\) with radius \(AB\) and the compass alone, divided the circumference into six equal parts. The other divisions they obtained with compass and straightedge, taking various points outside the circumference. Now we have found a point \(a\) such that it alone suffices to divide the circumference into twenty-four equal parts with the compass alone. Which is at once more expeditious and convenient, and leads to a practical division much more accurate than the ancient one.

35. The series of openings of the three compasses that suffice for this division may seem elegant: since the opening of the first is found to be \(= \sqrt{1}\), of the second \(= \sqrt{2}\), of the third \(= \sqrt{3}\).
