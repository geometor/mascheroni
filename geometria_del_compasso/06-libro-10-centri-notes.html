Commentary & Notes: Libro X — Napoleon Bonaparte and the Center of the Circle¶

Historical Legend, Mathematical Attribution & Projective Obstructions

1. The Legend of Napoleon’s Problem¶

The problem of finding the center of a given circle using only a compass has been known since the early nineteenth century as Napoleon’s Problem (problema di Napoleone).

According to historical traditions recorded in Paris and Milan:

  • General Bonaparte proposed this problem to French mathematicians at the Institut National in the winter of 1797–1798 following his Italian campaign.

  • Mascheroni had already published the exact solution in Pavia in 1797 as Proposition 143 of La Geometria del Compasso (mascheroni-1797, p. 82).

  • Whether Napoleon discovered his own derivation independently or communicated Mascheroni’s unpublished solution to Paris scholars, nineteenth-century European manuals permanently attached Bonaparte’s name to the challenge.

2. Projective Obstructions in Steiner’s Regime¶

The contrast between Mascheroni’s solution of Napoleon’s Problem and Jakob Steiner’s requirement for a given center highlights an essential theorem of projective geometry:

  • A straightedge alone can construct only projectively invariant properties (harmonic conjugates, collinearities, cross-ratios).

  • In the interior of a disk, the group of projective collineations mapping the circle onto itself is isomorphic to the isometry group of the hyperbolic plane (the Klein-Beltrami disk model).

  • Under this group, any interior point can be mapped to any other interior point while straight lines remain straight lines and the circumference remains the boundary.

  • Therefore, no straightedge construction can ever distinguish the Euclidean metric center \(M\) from any other interior point without an auxiliary metric witness!

  • The compass, by contrast, operates intrinsically within the Euclidean metric group, resolving the center unconditionally.
