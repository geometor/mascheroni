§§ 104–107: Half-Roots ½√1 through ½√25¶

104. Problem. To find (Fig. 45) easily the halves of the roots of the whole numbers from one to twenty-five.

Solution. Taking the radius \(AB\) equal to one, with center \(A\) describe the circle \(BDd\), and along its circumference make \(AB = BC = CD = DE = Ed\). With center \(B\) and radius \(BD\), describe an arc passing through the points \(a, N, D, d, n, \alpha\). With the same radius and center \(E\), describe an arc passing through the points \(a, M, C, m, \alpha\). With radius \(Aa\) and center \(B\), describe an arc passing through the points \(M, F, Q, q, m\).

f. 0079 · p. 54

With the same radius and center \(E\), describe an arc passing through the points \(N, F, P, p, n\).

With radius \(AB\) and center \(B\), describe an arc passing through \(P\) and \(p\).

With the same radius and center \(E\), describe an arc passing through \(Q\) and \(q\).

With the same radius and center \(P\), describe an arc passing through \(R\) and cutting the circumference at \(S\); with center \(p\), mark another arc cutting the first at \(R\) and the circumference at \(s\).

With the same radius and centers \(Q, q\), describe two arcs intersecting at \(T\) and cutting the circumference at \(O\) and \(o\).

With the same radius and center \(\alpha\), cut the circumference with an arc at \(g\). With center \(R\), cut the circumference with an arc at \(L\) and \(l\). With centers \(O\) and \(o\), mark two arcs intersecting at \(H\). With centers \(H\) and \(T\), mark two arcs intersecting at \(V\) and \(v\). We shall have

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} RA &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1} & HF &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{13} \\ RQ &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2} & EO &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{14} \\ RD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3} & Ll &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{15} \\ RP &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4} & BE &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{16} \\ RF &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5} & Ha &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{17} \\ AM &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6} & HN &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{18} \\ Qq &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7} & HD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{19} \\ Aa &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8} & ag &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{20} \\ BR &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{9} & dV &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{21} \\ BL &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{10} & HS &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{22} \\ pS &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{11} & Mm &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{23} \\ BD &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{12} & Mn &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{24} \\ HE &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{25}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Demonstration. If we compare the points \(P, B, p, R, E\) of this figure with the points \(A, p, B, P, Q\) of Fig. 3 by means of the equation of § 18, \((AQ)^2 = (Ap)^2 + pQ \cdot PQ\), we shall obtain the equation for this figure \((PE)^2 = (PB)^2 + BE \cdot RE\); that is, \((Aa)^2 = (AB)^2 + 2AB \cdot RE\); or (§ 27) \(2 = 1 + 2RE\). Hence \(RE = \frac{1}{2}AE\), and since \(R\) lies on the same straight line \(BAE\) (§ 13), we shall also have \(RA = \frac{1}{2}AE = \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1}\).

Since point \(T\) is at the midpoint of \(AB\) for the same

f. 0080 · p. 55

reason by which point \(R\) was proved to be at the midpoint of \(AE\), it will be \(AT = RE\); hence comparing the points \(Q, T, q, E, R\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(A, q, B, Q, P\) of Fig. 3, point \(A\) of Fig. 45 will be point \(p\) of Fig. 3. Therefore from the equation of § 16, \((AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ\), we shall obtain for this Fig. 45 the equation \((QE)^2 = (RQ)^2 + (RE)^2 + AR \cdot RE\); that is, substituting the numerical values, \(1 = (RQ)^2 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\). Hence \((RQ)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2\); \(RQ = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2}\).

Comparing the points \(D, A, d, E\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(P, A, p, B\) of Fig. 3, it will remain proved from § 14 that the two \(AE, Dd\) mutually bisect each other. But \(AE\) is bisected at \(R\); therefore \(Dd\) is also. But \(Dd = BD = \sqrt{3}\) (§ 2). Therefore \(RD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}\).

Note that \(DRd\) is also perpendicular to \(AE\) (§ 14).

Next we have \(RP = 1\). Therefore \(RP = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4}\).

Since the angle \(FAR\) is right (§ 27), we have \((RF)^2 = (FA)^2 + (AR)^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}\). Therefore \(RF = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}\).

Since the base \(BAE\) of the triangle \(BME\) is bisected by the straight line \(AM\), we shall have by § 26, \((BM)^2 + (EM)^2 = 2(AB)^2 + 2(AM)^2\); that is, \((Aa)^2 + (BD)^2 = 2(AB)^2 + 2(AM)^2\); that is, \(2 + 3 = 2 + 2(AM)^2\). Hence \(6 = 4(AM)^2\); \(\sqrt{6} = 2AM\); \(AM = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6}\).

Having compared above the points \(Q, T, q, E, R, A\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(A, q, B, Q, P, p\) of Fig. 3, it will follow from § 13 that in this Fig. 45 \(AQ = Aq = QR = Rq\). For the same reasons, the four \(AP, Ap, PT, Tp\) will turn out equal to one another and to these four. Therefore, since the two isosceles triangles \(PTA, QAR\) have all their sides equal to each other, the angle \(PAT = QRA\) (I.8), and since \(TAR\) is a straight line, \(PA\) will be parallel to \(QR\) (I.29). Hence \(PQ\) is also equal and parallel to \(AR\) (I.33).

But \(Qq\) is perpendicular to \(AR\) (§ 14). Therefore also to \(PQ\) (I.27). Next, in the two triangles \(PAT, RAq\), the two angles \(PAT, RAq\) are equal (I.8), and \(TAR\) is a straight line. Therefore, since the two angles \(PAT, PAR\) are equal to two right angles (I.13),

f. 0081 · p. 56

so also will be the two \(PAR, RAq\). Hence \(PAq\) will also be a straight line (I.14). Therefore \((Pq)^2 = (2RQ)^2 = (PQ)^2 + (Qq)^2\) (I.47); that is, \(2 = \frac{1}{4} + (Qq)^2\); hence \(\frac{7}{4} = (Qq)^2\), and \(Qq = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7}\).

Next we have \((Aa)^2 = 2\) (§ 27) \(= \frac{8}{4}\). Hence \(Aa = \frac{\sqrt{8}}{\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{8}\).

Also let \(BR = \frac{3}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{9}\).

If we compare the points \(B, L, R, l, A\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(Q, A, p, B, P\) of Fig. 3, from the equation \((AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ\) of § 16 we shall obtain for this Fig. 45 the equation \((BL)^2 = (LA)^2 + (AB)^2 + AR \cdot AB\); that is, \((BL)^2 = 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{10}{4}\). Hence \(BL = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{10}\).

The two triangles \(PSA, PBA\) have their sides respectively equal. Therefore the angle \(SPA = PAB\) (I.8). Hence \(PS\) and \(BA\) are parallel (I.28). But \(Pp\) cuts \(BR\) at right angles (§ 14). Therefore it will also be perpendicular to \(PS\) (I.27). Then in the same manner as \((Qq)^2 = \frac{7}{4}\) was proved, it will also be proved that \((Pp)^2 = \frac{7}{4}\). Hence since \((pS)^2 = (Pp)^2 + (PS)^2\) (I.47), we shall have \((pS)^2 = \frac{7}{4} + 1 = \frac{11}{4}\); hence \(pS = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{11}\).

Next we have \((BD)^2 = 3\) (§ 2) \(= \frac{12}{4}\). Hence \(BD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{12}\).

We also have \((HF)^2 = (HA)^2 + (AF)^2\); and since \(QO\) is proved parallel to \(BE\) in the same manner as was proved for \(PS\), the points \(O, P, Q, S\) will lie on the same straight line, and \(PO = QO - PQ = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} = PQ\). Therefore, since \(OP\) is equal and parallel both to \(TA\) and to \(TB\), \(OT, PA\) will also be equal and parallel, and the two \(OB, PT\) to each other (I.33). But it was proved above that \(PT = PA\). Therefore it will also be equal to these, \(= OT = OB\). For the same reason, on the other side \(oT, oB\) will be equal to \(pA = PA\). Now if we compare the points \(O, o, A, T, B, H\) with the points \(A, B, Q, P, p, q\) of Fig. 3, it will follow from § 14 that \(HB = AT = \frac{1}{2}\). Therefore \((HA)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 = \frac{9}{4}\); hence \((HF)^2 = \frac{9}{4} + 1 = \frac{13}{4}\) and \(HF = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{13}\).

If we compare the points \(E, O, H, o, A\) with the points \(Q, A, p, B, P\) of Fig. 3, from the equation \((AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ\) of § 16 we shall obtain for this Figure \((EO)^2 = (OA)^2 + (AE)^2 + HA \cdot AE = 1 + 1 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{7}{2} = \frac{14}{4}\). Hence \(EO = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{14}\).

Next, if we compare the points \(A, R, L, Q, q, l\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(A, B, Q, P, p, q\) of Fig. 3,

f. 0082 · p. 57

having (§ 15) the equation for Fig. 3, \((QM)^2 = (AQ)^2 - (AM)^2\), and multiplying by 4, \(4(QM)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - 4(AM)^2\), or \((Qq)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - (AB)^2\), we shall obtain for this Fig. 45, \((Ll)^2 = 4(AL)^2 - (AR)^2 = 4 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{15}{4}\); hence \(Ll = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{15}\).

Next we have \(BE = 2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{16}\).

By the right angle \(aAH\) (§ 27), we have \((Ha)^2 = (HA)^2 + (Aa)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 + 2 = \frac{17}{4}\); hence \(Ha = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{17}\).

Since it is proved in the same way that \((AN)^2 = \frac{3}{2}\), as was proved for \((AM)^2\), and likewise \((An)^2 = \frac{3}{2}\); because \(NR\) bisects the base \(AE\) of the triangle \(ANE\), we shall have (§ 26) \((AN)^2 + (NE)^2 = 2(AR)^2 + 2(RN)^2\); that is, \(\frac{3}{2} + 2 = \frac{2}{4} + 2(RN)^2\); and reducing we find \((RN)^2 = \frac{3}{2} = (AN)^2\). Likewise we find \((Rn)^2 = \frac{3}{2}\). Now if we compare the points \(H, N, R, n, A\) of this Fig. 45 with the points \(Q, A, p, B, P\) of Fig. 3, from the equation \((AQ)^2 = (AP)^2 + (PQ)^2 + Pp \cdot PQ\) of § 16 we shall obtain for Fig. 45 the equation \((HN)^2 = (AN)^2 + (AH)^2 + AR \cdot AH\), that is, \((HN)^2 = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{18}{4}\). Hence \(HN = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{18}\).

Since \(DR\) is perpendicular to \(AE\), that is, to \(HR\), we shall have \((HD)^2 = (HR)^2 + (RD)^2\) (I.47) \(= 4 + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{19}{4}\); hence \(HD = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{19}\).

Since the base \(aA\alpha\) of the triangle \(ag\alpha\) is bisected by the straight line \(gA\), we shall have (§ 26) \((ag)^2 + (\alpha g)^2 = 2(Aa)^2 + 2(Ag)^2\); that is, \((ag)^2 + 1 = 4 + 2\); and \((ag)^2 = 5 = \frac{20}{4}\); hence \(ag = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{20}\).

Since the two triangles \(HTV, AED\) have their sides equal to each other, the angle \(VTH = DEA\) (I.8), and since the points \(H, T, A, E\) lie on the same straight line, \(VT\) will be parallel to its equal \(DE\) (I.29), and hence \(VD\) is also parallel and equal to \(TE\) (I.33). But \(Dd\) is perpendicular to \(AE\), that is, to \(TE\); therefore also to \(VD\) (I.27). Hence \((dV)^2 = (VD)^2 + (Dd)^2 = (TE)^2 + (BD)^2 = (\frac{3}{2})^2 + 3\) (§ 2) \(= \frac{9}{4} + \frac{12}{4} = \frac{21}{4}\); hence \(dV = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{21}\).

Having proved \(PS\) equal and parallel to \(AE\), and \(ps\) for the same reason, \(SE\) will also be equal and parallel to \(AP\) (I.33), and likewise \(sE\) to \(Ap\). By the equality and parallelism of the three \(PS, TR, ps\), the equality of \(RS, Rs\) to \(PT, pT\), both proved equal to \(AP = RQ\), will be proved in the same way. Comparing now the points \(H, S, E, s, R\) of this Figure with the points \(Q, A, p, B, P\) of Fig. 3,

f. 0083 · p. 58

from the equation \((AQ)^2 = (Ap)^2 + pQ \cdot PQ\) (§ 18) we shall obtain for this Fig. 45, \((HS)^2 = (SE)^2 + EH \cdot RH = (RQ)^2 + EH \cdot RH = \frac{2}{4} + \frac{5}{2} \cdot 2 = \frac{22}{4}\). Hence \(HS = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{22}\).

Since the base \(AE\) of the triangle \(AME\) is bisected by \(MR\), we shall have (§ 26) \((AM)^2 + (EM)^2 = 2(RA)^2 + 2(RM)^2\); that is, \(\frac{6}{4} + 3 = \frac{2}{4} + 2(RM)^2\). Hence reducing we obtain \((RM)^2 = 2 = (BM)^2 = (Bm)^2\); of which value \((Rm)^2\) is proved to be in the same way. Hence \(BM = MR = Rm = mB\). Now if we compare the four points \(B, M, R, m\) of this Figure with the four points \(A, Q, B, q\) of Fig. 3, since from § 15 we have \((QM)^2 = (AQ)^2 - (AM)^2\), and hence \(4(QM)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - 4(AM)^2\), that is, \((Qq)^2 = 4(AQ)^2 - (AB)^2\), we shall have for this Fig. 45, \((Mm)^2 = 4(BM)^2 - (BR)^2 = 8 - (\frac{3}{2})^2 = \frac{32}{4} - \frac{9}{4} = \frac{23}{4}\); hence \(Mm = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{23}\).

Since the triangles \(BME, BnE\) have their sides equal to each other, and both have the common base \(BE\) bisected by \(AM, An\), from the equation of § 26 the same value will result for the two \(AM, An\). Therefore, since the triangles \(BAM, EAn\) have their sides equal to each other, the angle \(BAM = EAn\) (I.8). Hence, because of the straight line \(BAE\), since the sum of the two angles \(MAB, MAE\) equals two right angles (I.13), substituting for the angle \(MAB\) its equal \(EAn\), the sum of the two angles \(MAE, EAn\) will also equal two right angles. Therefore the two \(MA, An\) will form a single straight line (I.14). Thus \((Mn)^2 = 4(AM)^2 = \frac{24}{4}\). Hence \(Mn = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{24}\).

Finally \(HE = \frac{5}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{25}\).

105. Since \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{\frac{n}{4}}\)—for example, \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{7} = \sqrt{\frac{7}{4}}\), etc.— we shall easily have from this Problem the roots of all fourths from one fourth up to twenty-five fourths; which will be of use in constructing similar Figures, as we shall see.

106. If we had taken the radius \(AB = 2\), we would have obtained all these distances doubled in value; hence we would have had the whole-number roots of the numbers from one to twenty-five. However, we could easily double any of those half-roots that we wished, in order to obtain the whole root (§ 64).

107. The ease of this construction, which is executed with only the three compasses of the three openings already noted (§ 35)—the first \(= \sqrt{1}\), the third \(= \sqrt{2}\), the second \(= \sqrt{3}\)—with which the circle was already divided into 24 equal parts, and now 25
