§§ 69–70: Division into Any Number of Equal Parts¶

69. Prob. To divide a distance \(AB\) into any number of equal parts.

Sol. From an example or two the general rule will be better perceived (fig. 21).

Ex. I. Let the distance \(AB\) be to be divided into five equal parts. To it add in a straight line the four distances equal to it, \(AE, EF, FG, GH\) (§ 65), so that it remains quintupled in \(BH\), that is, multiplied by as many units as the number of parts into which it is to be divided. With the extremities \(B\) and \(H\) as centers, and with radius \(AB\) of the length of the distance to be divided, describe the two indefinite arcs \(AC, GI\). With the same centers \(B\) and \(H\), and with radius \(BH\), describe the two arcs \(HI, BC\). With center \(C\) and the first radius \(AB\) describe an indefinite arc \(BQ\). With radius \(CI\) and center \(H\) describe an arc cutting arc \(BQ\) at \(Q\). The distance \(BQ\) will lie along the direction of \(BA\), and will be the fifth part of it.

If now to \(BQ\) we add the equal \(Qq\) (§ 64), then the other equals \(qr, rs\), all the fifth parts of \(BA\) will be determined.

Ex. II. If it is desired to divide \(AB\) into seven parts (fig. 22);

form \(BH\) seven times greater than \(BA\). With the extremities of this, namely points \(B\) and \(H\), and with radius \(AB\), describe the indefinite arcs \(AC, GI\). With the same centers \(B\) and \(H\), and with radius \(BH\), describe the two arcs \(HI, BC\). With center \(C\) and the first radius \(AB\) describe an indefinite arc \(BQ\). With radius \(CI\) and center \(H\) cut this arc at \(Q\); \(BQ\) will lie along the direction of \(BA\), and will be the seventh part of it.

Dem. Since triangle \(CQI\) has sides respectively equal to triangle \(IHQ\), angle \(CIQ = IQH\) (I.8), hence \(CI\) is parallel to \(HQ\) (I.28). And since \(CI\) is also parallel to \(BH\) (§ 23), point \(Q\) will lie on \(BH\). Hence the two isosceles triangles \(CBQ, CBH\) having a common base angle at \(B\), they will be similar (I.5 and 32; VI.4), and \(HB : BC :: BC : BQ\), or \(HB : AB :: AB : BQ\). Therefore as many times as \(HB\) is greater than \(AB\), so many times will \(AB\) be greater than \(BQ\).

Sol. II. Let it be desired to divide \(AB\), for example, into five parts (fig. 23).

Having determined, as in Sol. I, \(BH\) five times greater than \(AB\), with center \(H\) and radius \(HB\)

describe an indeterminate arc \(CBc\). Now with radius \(BA\) and center \(B\) describe the semicircumference \(cCK\) (§ 64). With the same radius \(AB\) and center \(C\) describe the arc \(BQ\). With radius \(CK\) and center \(B\) cut this arc at \(Q\); \(BQ\) will be the fifth part of \(BA\) placed along the same direction.

Dem. The straight line \(BK\) will lie on the continuation of \(Bc\) (IV.15). Making therefore the due substitutions in § 22, we shall have \(KC \cdot BH = (BC)^2 = (AB)^2\); and hence (VI.17) \(BH : AB :: AB : KC\), or \(BH : AB :: AB : BQ\). As much, therefore, as \(BH\) is greater than \(AB\), so much will \(AB\) itself be greater than \(BQ\); and if \(BH = 5\,AB\), then also \(AB = 5\,BQ\). Moreover, from the mutual equality of the sides in the two triangles \(BKC, BCQ\), we shall have angle \(KCB = CBQ\) (I.8). But to angle \(KCB\) is also equal angle \(CBH\) (§ 22). Therefore \(BQ\) lies along the direction of \(BH\).

70. It is clearly seen that this last Problem (§ 69) can be very useful in practice for dividing into lines a given foot already divided into inches; since by its method, \(BH\) being equal to twelve inches, \(BQ\) will turn out equal to one twelfth of the first inch \(AB\), that is, to one line. In the same manner the meter already divided into decimeters can be subdivided into centimeters. If the straight line \(AB\), upon which point \(Q\) is to be found, has already been drawn, the operation will be simpler, since without describing arc \(BQ\) with center \(C\) and radius \(AB\), it will suffice to cut the given straight line \(AB\) at \(Q\) with the compass openings indicated above.
