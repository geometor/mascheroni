§§ 236–266: Arc-Minutes & French 400-Grad Decimal Circle
========================================================

.. folio:: 0158
   :page: 133

**236. Problem.** To find the arc of a quarter of a degree, that is, of :math:`15'`, without the error of a third-minute.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -12^\circ`; the distance :math:`ez` will be equal to the chord of :math:`87^\circ 15'`. If therefore it be taken on the compass, and with center at :math:`B` the arc :math:`BF` be cut at a point :math:`Z'`, the arc :math:`BZ'` will be :math:`87^\circ 15'`.
Then we have from the divisions of § 42 the arc of :math:`87^\circ = \frac{29}{120}` of the circumference. We shall therefore have the difference of the two arcs :math:`= 15'`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (3) (§ 207) :math:`\cos E' = \sin 45^\circ - \sin 30^\circ + \cos(E - 22^\circ 30') - \cos(E + 22^\circ 30')` we set :math:`E = -12^\circ`, we shall have

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   \sin 45^\circ - \sin 30^\circ &= 0.2071068 \\
   \cos(-34^\circ 30') &= 0.8241262 \\
   -\cos 10^\circ 30' &= -1 + 0.0167451 \\
   \hline
   \cos E' &= 0.0479781
   \end{aligned}

Now in the tables that give the natural sines to seven digits, one finds :math:`0.0479781 = \cos 87^\circ 15'` without any variation even in the last digit. Using more decimals one finds :math:`\cos E' = 0.0479780622`; :math:`\cos 87^\circ 15' = 0.047978128520`.

.. folio:: 0159
   :page: 134

Using only eight decimal digits one has :math:`\cos 87^\circ 15' 1'' = 0.04797229`.

We have therefore for :math:`1''` the difference :math:`584`. If :math:`584` gives a difference of :math:`60'''`, :math:`7` will give :math:`\frac{105}{146}` of a third-minute. The arc :math:`E'`, of which :math:`eZ'` is chord, will therefore be :math:`87^\circ 15'` with a defect of less than one third-minute.

**237.** By means of this problem one will be able to divide the circumference into 1440 parts, that is, into so many quarters of a degree, without there being at any point of division an error of three third-minutes.
For having made upon each arc :math:`P\delta` of :math:`1^\circ 30'` three divisions of :math:`15'` by :math:`15'` from :math:`P` toward :math:`\delta` and two from :math:`\delta` toward :math:`P`, the arc :math:`P\delta` will remain divided into six parts, each of which will be a quarter of a degree without the indicated error in the position of any point of division. So in the rest of the circumference. Henceforth we shall suppose the circumference divided into degrees and quarters, which for simplicity of calculation we shall suppose exact. One can take account of their errors whenever desired.

**238. Problem.** To find an arc of :math:`10'`, or the sixth part of a degree, without the error of :math:`10'''`, or of the sixth part of a second.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -49^\circ 30'`; the distance :math:`bz` will be equal to the chord of the arc :math:`38^\circ 50'`, without the indicated error.
Subtracting then the arc :math:`38^\circ 50'` from the arc :math:`39^\circ = \frac{13}{120}` of the circumference, which is had by § 42, there will remain the arc of :math:`10'`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (2) we set :math:`B = -49^\circ 30'`, there will result :math:`\cos B' = 0.7789738 = \cos 38^\circ 49' \frac{1819}{1824}`. We have therefore :math:`B' = 38^\circ 50'` with a defect of :math:`9'''`.

**239.** Subtracting from an arc of :math:`50'` lacking :math:`9'''` an arc of :math:`45'` (§ 237) lacking a few third-minutes, we shall have the arc of :math:`5'`, or the twelfth part of the degree, without the error of :math:`9'''`.

**240. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`6'`, or one-tenth of a degree, without the error of :math:`13'''`.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`BZ = 45^\circ`, that is, let it be the arc :math:`BG`. Take on the compass the distance :math:`BG`, and with center at :math:`b` cut the circumference at :math:`z`. The arc :math:`Bz` will be :math:`-40^\circ 6'`, without the indicated error.
Subtracting the arc of :math:`-40^\circ` (§ 237), there will remain the arc of :math:`6'`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (5) we set :math:`B' = 45^\circ`, there will result

:math:`\sin B = -0.6441228 = \sin\left(-40^\circ 5' \frac{2217}{2225}\right)`. The arc :math:`B = Bz` will therefore be :math:`-40^\circ 6'` with a defect of :math:`12'''`.

**242. Problem.** To find immediately the arc of :math:`1'` without the error of :math:`22` third-minutes.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -27^\circ`. Take on the compass the distance :math:`bz` as radius, and with center at :math:`e` cut the circumference at :math:`Z`. The arc :math:`BZ` will be :math:`29^\circ 59'` with an excess of :math:`10` third-minutes. Since therefore the arc :math:`BN = 30^\circ` (§ 31), the arc :math:`ZN` will be :math:`1'` lacking :math:`10'''`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (12) we set :math:`B = -27^\circ`, we shall have :math:`\sin E = 0.4997496 = \sin 29^\circ 59' \frac{15}{2519}`. Therefore, etc.

**243. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`9'` without the error of :math:`7` third-minutes.

*Sol.* With the distance :math:`bK` taken as radius, and with center at :math:`e`, mark an arc cutting the circumference at :math:`z`.
The arc :math:`Bz` will be :math:`-4^\circ 21'` with an excess of :math:`6` third-minutes, which subtracted from :math:`-4^\circ 30'` leaves :math:`9'` without the indicated error.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (12) we set :math:`B = 15^\circ = BK` (§ 32), we shall have :math:`\sin E = -0.0758494 = \sin\left(-4^\circ 21' \frac{5}{2901}\right)`. The arc :math:`Bz` will therefore be :math:`-4^\circ 21' 0'' 6'''`.

This problem will also serve for the new division of the circle, as we shall see (§ 257).

**244.** The arc of :math:`15'` lacking less than a third-minute (§ 236), that of :math:`10'` exceeding by less than :math:`10'''` (§ 238), that of :math:`6'` lacking less than :math:`13'''` (§ 240), that of :math:`1'` lacking less than :math:`22'''` (§ 242), and that of :math:`9'` lacking less than :math:`7'''` (§ 243) can be combined by addition or subtraction
in such a way without greatly accumulating the errors that in the end the whole circumference results divided into degrees and prime minutes without an error of more than a very few third-minutes. For by doubling, for example, the arc of :math:`9'`, we shall have an arc of :math:`18'` lacking less than :math:`14'''`, from which subtracting the arc of :math:`6'` lacking less than :math:`13'''`, we shall have the arc of :math:`12'` lacking about one third-minute.

.. folio:: 0161
   :page: 136

Subtracting now this arc from the arc of :math:`15'` lacking less than :math:`1'''` (§ 236), we shall have the arc of :math:`3'` with an error of scarcely one third-minute. With this one will be able to divide every arc of :math:`15'` into five parts,
and the whole circumference will remain divided by three and three prime minutes with an error less than six third-minutes on account of this last division; which error, if placed on the side opposite to the error of at most three third-minutes committed in dividing by :math:`15'` (§ 236), will become even smaller. Hence employing the arc of :math:`1'` lacking less than :math:`4'''` (§ 241) to divide each arc of :math:`3'`, one will never commit an error of :math:`10'''`, and will have the whole circumference divided into degrees and prime minutes.

One could also combine these, or other arcs drawn from the twelve equations above, in such a way that less error would be committed, and we would employ several problems on this, if on the one hand this division of the circle into :math:`360^\circ` were not destined to grow obsolete, and if on the other we believed that artisans who still wished to use it would find such researches opportune for practice.
We do not wish, however, to omit the following problems, supposing now that the entire circle has been divided into ancient degrees and minutes, which for simplicity we shall suppose exact.

**245. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`20''`, or a third of a minute, exceeding by scarcely :math:`1'''`.

*Sol. I.* By § 202 one finds the arc of :math:`40''` exceeding by less than one third-minute. Hence also the arc of :math:`20''`, complement to :math:`1'`, lacking less than one third-minute.

*Sol. II.* Having taken on the compass the chord of the arc :math:`61^\circ 30'` as radius, and with center at :math:`e`, describe an arc cutting the circumference at :math:`Z`. The arc :math:`BZ` will be :math:`20^\circ 39' 40''` exceeding by scarcely one third-minute.
Hence subtracting this arc from the arc :math:`20^\circ 40'`, we shall have the arc of :math:`20''` lacking scarcely :math:`1'''`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (6) we set :math:`E' = 61^\circ 30'`, using fuller tables of sines, we shall have

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   \sin BZ = \sin E &= 0.35283991; \quad \text{then one has} \\
   \sin 20^\circ 39' 40'' &= 0.35283984 \\
   \text{Now} \quad \log 0.3528399 &= 9.5475777 \\
   \log \sin 20^\circ 39' 40'' &= 9.5475776
   \end{aligned}

.. folio:: 0162
   :page: 137

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   \log \sin 20^\circ 39' 50'' &= 9.5476334 \\
   \text{difference} &= 558
   \end{aligned}

Therefore :math:`E` will exceed the arc :math:`20^\circ 39' 40''` by about :math:`\frac{10''}{558}`, that is, by :math:`1'''` and a little more.

**246. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`15''`, or a quarter of a minute, lacking about :math:`10'''`.

*Sol.* Take as radius on the compass the chord of :math:`31^\circ 30'`, and with center at :math:`e` cut the circumference at point :math:`Z`. The arc :math:`BZ` will be :math:`57^\circ 30' 15''` lacking :math:`9'''`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (6) we introduce :math:`E' = 31^\circ 30'`, we shall find :math:`E = 57^\circ 30' \frac{386}{1563}`. But we have :math:`\frac{392'}{1568} = \frac{1}{4}`. Therefore the defect is about :math:`\frac{4}{1563}`, that is, about :math:`10'''`.

**247. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`12''`, or a fifth of a minute, lacking about :math:`1'''`.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -10^\circ 30'`. Having taken on the compass as radius the distance :math:`bz`, and with center at :math:`a`, cut the circumference at :math:`Z`. The arc :math:`BZ` will be :math:`40^\circ 40' 12''` with a defect of about :math:`1'''`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (7) we set :math:`B = -10^\circ 30'`, there will result from the calculation :math:`A = 40^\circ 40' \frac{440}{2206}`. Now we have :math:`\frac{441}{2205} = \frac{1}{5}`. We have therefore the defect of :math:`\frac{1}{2206}`, that is, of about :math:`1'''`.

If one calculated with fuller tables, the error would be ascertained more precisely.

**248. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`10''`, or a sixth of a prime minute, exceeding by about :math:`1'''`.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -24^\circ`. Having taken on the compass as radius the distance :math:`ez`, and with center at :math:`a`, mark an arc cutting the circumference at :math:`Z`. The arc :math:`BZ` will be :math:`= 16^\circ 15' 10''` with the indicated error.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (9) we set :math:`E = -24^\circ`, there will come out as result :math:`\log \sin A = 9.4469652`. This is found to be the logarithm of the sine of :math:`16^\circ 15' 10'' \frac{20}{722}`. The excess therefore does not reach two third-minutes.

**249. Problem.** To find the arc of :math:`5''`, or of one-twelfth of a prime minute, without an error sensible to common tables and less than :math:`2'''`.

*Sol. I.* Let the arc :math:`BZ = 4^\circ 30'`. Take on the compass as radius the distance :math:`eZ`, and with center at :math:`a` mark an arc cutting the circumference at another point :math:`Z'`. The arc :math:`BZ'` will be :math:`32^\circ 51' 5''` without the indicated error.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (9) we set :math:`E = 4^\circ 30'`, there will result :math:`\log \sin A = 9.7343692`; now :math:`\log \sin 32^\circ 51' = 9.7343529`; and the difference is :math:`163`; then the difference in the tables for ten minutes is :math:`326`, exactly double :math:`163`. Therefore, etc.

Calculated with fuller tables, the error turns out less than :math:`2'''`.

*Sol. II.* Let the arc :math:`BZ = 31^\circ 30'`.
Take on the compass as radius the distance :math:`eZ`, and with center :math:`a` cut the circumference at another point :math:`Z'`. The arc :math:`BZ'` will be :math:`= 51^\circ 30' 55''` with a defect of less than one third-minute.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (9) we set :math:`E = 31^\circ 30'`, there will result by means of the common tables :math:`\log \sin A = 9.8936365 = \log \sin 51^\circ 30' 50'' \frac{84}{167}`. Which fraction :math:`\frac{84}{167}`, referring to :math:`10''`, will give :math:`5''` with an excess of less than :math:`1'''`.

Therefore :math:`A = 51^\circ 30' 55''`, which arc subtracted from :math:`51^\circ 31'` will leave :math:`5''` with the indicated defect.

But it is time to pass on to showing what use can be made of the twelve equations set down above when wishing to divide the circumference of the circle in the new manner of the French.

**250.** According to this manner, the circumference is divided into 400 degrees, so that the quadrant, which is the foundation of all trigonometry, remains divided into 100 degrees. Each degree is divided into 100 prime minutes, each prime minute into 100 seconds, and so forth. The nature of decimals dispenses, if one wishes, from naming degrees, minutes, or seconds, the nature of the fraction being sufficiently understood from the place of the decimals.

**251.** Hence it follows that nine ancient degrees are worth 10 modern degrees, that is, :math:`9^\circ = 0.10`; that :math:`54' = 0.01`; that :math:`27' = 0.005`; that :math:`5' 24'' = 0.001`; that :math:`32' 24''' = 0.0001`; that is, that one modern degree is worth :math:`54` ancient prime minutes, that one modern minute is worth :math:`32' 24'''` of the ancient division, etc.

.. folio:: 0164
   :page: 139

**252.** The accurate divisions obtained by means of the three points :math:`a`, :math:`b`, and :math:`e` in the Second Book give as far as a :math:`240^{\text{th}}` of the circumference (§ 59). The arc that forms it is in the ancient division :math:`1^\circ 30'` exactly. It is not equally expressed by a finite number of decimal digits in the modern division.
The first arc formed by aggregating the :math:`240^{\text{ths}}` that is expressed by a finite number of decimals of the quadrant is the arc of :math:`\frac{3}{240}`, or of :math:`\frac{1}{80}` of the circumference, which is :math:`4^\circ 30' = 0.05` of the quadrant, that is, :math:`5` degrees of the new division. One can therefore by the methods of the Second Book, and by means of the three points :math:`a`, :math:`b`, and :math:`e` alone taken outside the circumference, divide it into equal parts of five modern degrees each, and this with geometrical precision, which is already a great advantage of this Geometry.

**253.** One could, if one wished, by bisection of arcs (§ 60), subsequently divide the circumference into arcs of two degrees and a half each, that is, of :math:`0.025`, and then continue the bisection, yet with it one will not be able to have, as is clear, one degree precisely. There remains therefore no means in Geometry to obtain the arc of one degree (§ 63), and we have only to seek some construction that may give it at least approximately.

**254. Problem.** To find the arc of a new degree, that is, of :math:`0.01`, without the excess of a sixth of a second of the new division, or of three third-minutes of the old.

*Sol.* Take on the compass the chord of :math:`138^\circ` degrees of the old division, or forty-six one-hundred-twentieth parts of the circumference (§ 42), and with center at :math:`a` mark an arc cutting the quadrant :math:`Bf` at a point :math:`z`.
The arc :math:`Bz` will be eleven degrees of the new division, from which subtracting the arc of :math:`9^\circ = 0.10` (§ 252), there will remain an arc :math:`= 0.01` with the very small excess indicated.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (4) we set :math:`A' = 138^\circ`, we shall have :math:`\sin A = \frac{1}{2} [\sin 45^\circ + \sin 183^\circ + \sin(-93^\circ)] = \frac{1}{2}(\sin 45^\circ - \sin 3^\circ - \sin 87^\circ)`. Then we have

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   -\sin 3^\circ &= -0.0523360 \\
   -\sin 87^\circ &= -0.9986295 \\
   \sin 45^\circ &= 1 - 0.2928932 \\
   \hline
   &\phantom{=} -0.3438587
   \end{aligned}

.. folio:: 0165
   :page: 140

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   &\phantom{=} -0.1719293 \\
   \text{Then we have} \quad \sin 9^\circ 57' &= 0.1719291 \\
   \sin 9^\circ 58' &= 0.1722156
   \end{aligned}

We have therefore the arc :math:`Bz = A = 9^\circ 57'` with the sole excess of :math:`\frac{2}{2865}` of a prime minute of the old division, that is, without the excess of :math:`4'''`, and hence without the error of one-sixth of a second of the new division.

**255.** With tables somewhat fuller than common ones, such an error could be investigated more precisely.
In any case it is so small that even by accumulating it two or three times it would not become sensible even in the largest quadrants. Now there will be no need to accumulate it more than twice in dividing them. For we already have with geometrical precision the arc of :math:`0.05` (§ 252). If upon this one marks two divisions of one degree beginning from the two ends, and coming in the opposite direction, there will be marked on this arc four points, which will give its division into five degrees,
and each of these points will not be distant from its true position by six whole third-minutes of the old division, or by one-third of a second of the new.

**256.** By virtue of this problem, the circumference will now be supposed divided into the 400 degrees of the new division.

**257. Problem.** To find the arc of a new half-degree without the excess of seven third-minutes of the old division, or of one-third of a second of the new.

*Sol.* Take on the compass the distance from point :math:`b` to point :math:`K`, and with this radius :math:`bK`, center at :math:`e`, mark an arc cutting the circumference at a point :math:`z`. The arc :math:`Bz` will be :math:`= -4^\circ 21'` with an excess less than :math:`7'''`. Subtract from it an arc of :math:`3^\circ = Np` (§ 43). There will remain :math:`1^\circ 21'`,
that is, one degree and a half of the new division, with whose chord taken as radius, and centering at all the points of the degrees (§ 256), all the same degrees can be divided in half.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (12) we introduce :math:`B = BK = 15^\circ` (§ 32), there will result :math:`E = -4^\circ 21' \frac{5}{2901}`. Therefore, etc. (§ 243).

**258. Problem.** To find the arc of a fifth of a new degree without the excess of one old second.

*Sol.* Taking on the compass the chord of :math:`51^\circ`, and with center at :math:`e`, mark an arc cutting the quadrant at :math:`Z`; the arc :math:`BZ` will be thirty-seven new degrees with the addition of a fifth of a degree with the small indicated error.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (6) we set :math:`E' = 51^\circ`, we shall have :math:`E = 33^\circ 28' \frac{1968}{2426} = 33^\circ 28' 48'' \frac{1632}{2426}`. But :math:`33^\circ 28' 48'' = 0.372`. Therefore, etc.

**259. Problem.** To find the arc of four-tenths of a new degree without the excess of :math:`16'''` ancient.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`BV = 76^\circ 30'`, and take a compass opening :math:`= bZ`, with which, centering at :math:`a`, mark an arc cutting the quadrant at a point :math:`Z'`. The arc :math:`BZ'` will be :math:`= 0.094`, that is, nine new degrees and four-tenths with the indicated excess.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (7) we introduce :math:`B = 76^\circ 30'`, there will come :math:`A = 8^\circ 27' \frac{1738}{2877}`. But :math:`8^\circ 27' \frac{1726}{2877} = 0.094`. Therefore, etc.

**260. Problem.** To divide a degree of the new division into ten equal parts.

*Sol.* Having divided it in half by means of § 257, subtract from the arc of :math:`0.005` the arcs of :math:`0.004` (§ 259) and of :math:`0.002` (§ 258), and we shall have the arcs of :math:`0.001` and of :math:`0.003` with the error of only third-minutes of the old division. We shall therefore have all the arcs for dividing the arc of :math:`0.005` into five parts, and thus dividing similarly the other half of the degree, the whole will remain divided into ten equal parts.

**261.** One can make subtractions and sums of the aforesaid arcs in such a way that, setting off the excesses against the defects, an almost exact thousandth results. One may therefore henceforth suppose the quadrant divided into thousandths, that is, by tens of new minutes. These will be supposed exact for simplicity of calculation.

**262. Problem.** To find the arc of a new minute without the error of an old third-minute.

*Sol.* Let there be an arc :math:`Bz = -1^\circ 30'`. The distance :math:`az` will be the chord

   :page: 142

of an arc of :math:`1.3609` without the indicated error. Subtracting this arc from the arc of :math:`1.361` (§ 260), we shall have the arc of :math:`0.0001`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (1) we set :math:`A = -1^\circ 30'`, there results :math:`A' = 122^\circ 28' \frac{2111}{2454} = 122^\circ 28' 51'' 36''' = 1.3609` (§ 251). Therefore, etc.

**263. Problem.** To find the arc of two new minutes without the error of an old third-minute.

*Sol.* Let the arc :math:`Bz = -48^\circ`. The distance :math:`bz` will be the chord of an arc of :math:`0.4422` with the indicated precision. From this arc subtracting the arc of :math:`0.442` (§ 260), there will remain the arc of :math:`0.0002`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation (2) we set :math:`B = -48^\circ`, there results :math:`B' = 39^\circ 47' \frac{1639}{1862} = 39^\circ 47' 52'' 48''' = 0.4422` (§ 251). Therefore, etc.

**264. Problem.** To find the arc of three new minutes without the error of a new third-minute.

*Sol.* Let there be an arc :math:`Bz = -78^\circ`. With the distance :math:`ez` taken as radius, and with center at :math:`a`, mark an arc cutting the quadrant :math:`Bf` at a point :math:`z'`. The arc :math:`Bz'` will be :math:`= -0.0187` without the indicated error. If this arc be subtracted from the arc :math:`= -0.019` (§ 260), there will remain the arc :math:`= -0.0003`.

*Demonstration.* If in equation [9] we set :math:`E = -78^\circ`, the sine of :math:`A` comes out negative. If we change the signs on both sides of the equation, there results :math:`\log \sin A = 8.4678991`. Now in the new Tables of Callet for the new division of the circle one finds :math:`8.4678990 = \log \sin 0.0187`.

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   \text{From } \log \sin A &= 8.4678991 \\
   \text{subtract } DS &= 6.1960574 \quad (\text{See Callet}) \\
   \hline
   \text{we get } 2.2718417 &= \log 187.0000.
   \end{aligned}

We have therefore :math:`A = -0.018700004`. The error is found to be even smaller if more digits are used in the logarithms.

**265.** The extreme closeness in the position of the point in these three preceding problems, and especially in the last, is such that nothing more can be desired. By means of the arcs found with these problems one can in several ways divide a thousandth of a quadrant (§ 260) into ten equal parts, that is, into prime minutes of the new division of the circle.

**266.** In the same manner one could pass to more minute divisions. But it would be necessary to employ tables fuller in digits than those which I have generally employed in calculating the

twelve equations mentioned above. This could be done whenever use required that the division of a circumference be pushed beyond the prime minutes of the new French system.
