===========================================
Fazzari's Biographical Preface (pp. III–IX)
===========================================

.. folio:: 0016
   :page: III

Lorenzo Mario Mascheroni dell'Olmo was born at Castagneta near Bergamo on May 13, 1750, to Paolo and Maria Ceribelli (1). Having entered the Seminary schools of Bergamo as a boy, he attended them until his eighteenth year as a lay pupil,
always earning the applause of his teachers both for his talent and for his diligence: in 1768 he took the habit of a cleric, and six years later he was ordained a priest.

Not yet twenty years old, his biographers relate that he succeeded his teacher Ottavio Bolgeni in the chair of rhetoric at the same Seminary:
on August 5, 1773, he was appointed to the same teaching post in the *Collegio Mariano*, where the public schools of Bergamo were located.

During this period he cultivated polite literature, and it was in his twenty-sixth year that he turned his vast and profound mind to the studies of philosophy and mathematics.
He himself used to say that his first steps in the study of exact science cost him no small labor; yet with patience and the acuteness of his intellect he happily overcame all difficulties.
And in 1778 he was appointed Lecturer in philosophy in the same schools of Bergamo, a teaching post that comprised logic, metaphysics, and physics, to which he added elementary mathematics first and then integral and differential calculus, something wholly new for those schools.

.. folio:: 0017
   :page: IV

However, the envious on one side and the reactionaries on the other, since he had dared to go against the hitherto permanent system of blind devotion to the dictates of the Stagirite and to follow in the footsteps of Galileo, waged war upon him,
and the Council that presided over the public schools of Bergamo began by its deliberations to hamper his freedom of teaching, so much so that in 1786 he was forced to resign his post
and go to Pavia, where in that University he found a field more propitious to his genius (2). There, succeeding the illustrious Paoli, he taught first the elements of Algebra and Geometry and afterwards applied Mathematics;
and in the academic years 1789–90 and 1793–94 he also held the office of Rector.

When French troops entered Milan on May 6, 1796, and the University of Pavia closed briefly, upon its reopening Mascheroni was reconfirmed in his chair;
in 1797 he was first elected to the *Società di Pubblica Istruzione* and then to the *Corpo legislativo consulente*, which, once the Cisalpine Republic was proclaimed, presided for a time over public affairs.
In the same year he was sent by Bonaparte first to Valle Camonica as Commissioner of the *Executive Directory* to establish the boundaries between the Departments of the Serio and the Mella,
and then named among the Representatives of the people in the Grand Council and reconfirmed for the following year.

The government of the French Republic having appointed a Commission of mathematicians to study a new system of weights and measures
deduced from the magnitude of the Earth, on May 25, 1798, the Directory invited Mascheroni to Paris to take part in the work of this commission;
and on September 17 of the same year he went to the capital of France, where he won the esteem and goodwill not only of his illustrious colleagues on the commission, but of all the scholars and scientists who came near him. There, however, soon began for Mascheroni the sorrows that led him to the grave.
When the Austro-Russians entered Milan on April 14, 1799, and the Cisalpine Republic fell, he found himself without the stipends coming to him from Italy, and was forced to seek a livelihood by accepting the teaching of Physics and Mathematics at the College of Antoine Dubois, to which he had been recommended by Lagrange.
He taught there four months, from October 13, 1799, to March 9, 1800, when, both through the grief of knowing his dear homeland under Austrian despotism and through the changed way of life and the fatigue of teaching, he fell gravely ill.

.. folio:: 0018
   :page: V

After the battle of Marengo, having already been reappointed by Bonaparte, First Consul of the French Republic, to his chair at the University of Pavia, he hoped to recover soon and return to Italy, but the illness would not spare him, and on July 14, 1800, of an obstinate chest ailment, at fifty years of age, that *gentle soul*

| *After much striving within its veil*
| *And weary longing for release, at last*
| *Spread its wings, and shining rose to heaven.*

He died, Mongili wrote, as the upright man dies who lived to give examples of virtue and to increase the sacred treasury of scientific knowledge.


He was buried in Paris with honors worthy of him; yet, a grievous fact, today it is unknown where the mortal remains of the gentle poet and keen Bergamasque mathematician rest.

In Ravelli's *Bibliografia mascheroniana* is found an extensive *List of the biographies, eulogies, and notices* of our Author (3).
Other biographical notices may be gathered from Fiammazzo's volume and from the works cited there. Without dwelling on these notices, we shall give here the list of Mascheroni's mathematical works with a brief account of them, passing over his literary and political works altogether.

1. *On the finest property of the isochronous curve with converging directions, etc.*

This is a brief note published on September 19, 1782, and dedicated to the mathematician Achille Alessandri of Bergamo, wherein it is shown that the isochronous curve tends toward an infinite spiral making a constant angle with the radius vector. It is Mascheroni's first mathematical work.

2. In the same year, 1782, he published a little pamphlet on the *Manner of measuring the inclination of the magnetic needle* (Bergamo, 1782, in-8vo with one plate).

At that time only an inexact method of Musschenbroek was known for measuring the intensity of magnetic force at a given place.

.. folio:: 0019
   :page: VI

In this study Mascheroni suggests suspending the magnetic needle at a right angle to the lower end of a vertical copper rod so that it may oscillate very easily according to the varying magnetic inclination at different places, or at the same place at different times.
When the needle is placed in the local magnetic meridian, it does not stay horizontal, but one end inclines northward and by its oscillations describes a transcendental curve whose ordinates, as Mascheroni proves, are proportional to the magnetic force. He describes the curve's nature, closely analogous to Nicomedes' conchoid and Johann Bernoulli's epicycloid, studies its singular points, gives the rule for drawing tangents, and determines its quadrature.

3. *On the curves that serve to delineate the unequal hours of the ancients on plane surfaces.*

This brief note was first inserted in vol. VII of the *Opuscoli scelti sulle scienze e sulle arti* (Milan, 1784) and then reprinted in the second edition of the following work.

In it he proved accurate the observation also made by Abbé Scipione Dehe, that the unequal hours of the Jews and Romans ought not to be marked on plane surfaces by straight lines; and to illustrate this he engraved a *Sundial* on a brass disc, now owned by the Civic Library of Bergamo, showing the hour lines of the various ancient systems.

4. *New Researches on the Equilibrium of Vaults.*

This work, which is generally held to be his chief one and through which the Author soon rose to fame as an able geometer, was first printed in Bergamo in 1785 (in-4to, with 13 plates) and then with the eulogy of Marquis Ferdinando Landi in Milan in 1829 (in-16mo, with 5 plates).

In it he proves himself not only a master of Mechanics and of the Geometry of the ancients, but also of the infinitesimal Calculus; and although the subject had already been treated by able mathematicians such as Jakob Bernoulli, Gregory, La Hire, and Lorgna, he remains original and adds new and important truths on the subject.
Considering that matter cannot be held perfectly rigid and uniformly hard, and that therefore the intrados is not a true curve of equilibrium, he transfers this to the line passing through the centers of gravity of all the voussoirs forming the vault, studies the surface of equilibrium, the locus of these lines in the various vertical sections, and, through the principle of virtual velocities, creates the science of the solid foundation of buildings, examining equilibrium in every species of vaults.

.. folio:: 0020
   :page: VII

For this work he received praise from the mathematicians of the age, and Bossut wrote to him: "You have treated in this work a great number of very interesting and very useful questions; and you show therein great sagacity in handling analysis. You seem to me destined to honor your country."

5. In the second Italian edition (Pavia, 1787) of Abbé Bossut's course of mathematics translated by Father Andrea Mozzone—a textbook in very many Italian schools and also the one used by Mascheroni for his lectures on elementary mathematics at the University of Pavia—he inserted some notes of his own in an Appendix, notes later reproduced, in whole or in part, in other editions of the same Course.
Among the notes is found the *Method of measuring plane polygons*, which was also published separately in Pavia in the same year.

Here he teaches how to measure any polygon, convex or concave, without using the resolution of the polygon into triangles, determining certain elements from other given data.
At the end is the description of a sighting rule, by which one obtains not only the measure of the angle, but also its sine and cosine. Besides its novelty, one must admire in this pamphlet the order and clarity that are indeed found in all of Mascheroni's works.

L'Huilier inserted the problems and solutions of this little work in his *Polygonométrie*, published two years later (Geneva, 1789), without mentioning the Italian mathematician.
Mascheroni nobly alludes to this in the preface to his *Problems for Surveyors*, in the following words which we transcribe because they reveal his noble spirit: "I saw on reading this book (L'Huilier's) not only that my method contained all his problems, but moreover that in the analytical solutions I presented the same formulas and followed the same tracks as an author who had printed his book two years after mine:
and I truly marveled at seeing myself coincide in such a way with that mathematician." And he adds: "Notwithstanding this, M. L'Huilier's book still deserves that you avail yourself of it, both for its erudition and for the geometric demonstrations added by him."

   :page: VIII

6. *Adnotationes ad calculum integralem Euleri in quibus nonnulla problemata ab Eulero proposita resolventur, etc.* (Ticini, 1790).

7. *Adnotationum ad calculum integralem Euleri in quibus nonnullae formulae ab Eulero propositae plenius evolvuntur. Pars altera, etc.* (Ticini, 1792).

These two small volumes, in which the Author increasingly displays the acuteness of his genius, aim to solve various questions proposed by the greatest mathematician of the eighteenth century, and especially noteworthy are the researches on the so-called logarithmic integral.

8. At the end of the second volume of the second Verona edition of the works of Wolff (4), published in 1791, are the *Adnotationes Mascheronii in R. Archigymnasio Ticinensi Mathem. Prof.*

They are fifteen notes, all referring to the mechanical-static part of the work.

9. *Problems for Surveyors with Various Solutions.*

This work, which contributes to the *geometry of the straightedge*, was first published at Pavia in 1793 (in-8vo with 4 plates) and then at Milan in 1802 under the title: *Problems of Geometry with Demonstrations Added by Citizen Sacchi, Captain Second Class of Artillery, etc.* (in-8vo with 4 plates).

The demonstrations, as Sacchi himself states in his preface, were suggested by Mascheroni himself.

Of the same work, another edition appeared at Milan in 1832 under the title: *Problems of Geometry with Demonstrations by Captain Sacchi, an edition enriched by the addition of certain problems drawn from a copy of the first edition annotated by the author* (in-16mo, with 5 plates).

Moreover, this work was translated into French by an anonymous writer and published in Paris in 1803.

In the hope that a new edition may also be made of this book, which can be studied by our youths in secondary schools, we give here a brief summary of it.

The work consists of five books. In the first, which comprises altimetry, the Author gives methods for measuring the distance of inaccessible points;
in the second he gives methods for measuring angles and for drawing orthogonal straight lines without squares or graphometers; the third contains problems on measuring plane or curved surfaces and dividing them into parts standing in a given ratio to one another;
in the fourth he sets forth his polygonometry already published in the pamphlet mentioned above (no. 5), generalizing the formulas for any space enclosed by any number of straight lines; in the fifth, finally, he treats the measurement of polyhedral solids.

.. folio:: 0022
   :page: IX

10. In vol. II (June 1795 issue, pp. 185–206) of the *Giornale fisico-medico of L. Brugnatelli*, which was published in Pavia, there is by Mascheroni a
*Letter to the illustrious Signor Don Annibale Beccaria, Milanese patrician, with certain geometric problems solved with the circle without the rule.*

It is the first essay of the *Geometry of the Compass*, as the Author states in his introduction to this work (see p. 6 of the present edition), and contains among other constructions the division of the circumference with the compass alone into 24 and into 120 equal parts.
The first, being very simple, has been retained in the present work; the second, being complicated, was replaced by the Author with a much simpler one.

11. Two years later he brought to light the work *La Geometria del compasso* (Pavia, at the heirs of Pietro Galeazzi, Year V of the French Republic, 1797, in-8vo with 14 plates), which we reprint in full in this new edition.

He conceived this *original and curious* work (5), which made his name glorious among all mathematicians, in order to come to the aid of makers of astronomical instruments, since they managed to divide the circumference into equal parts by repeated trials, even making use of scales and double planes, yet without reaching the desired geometric precision.
By means of three points geometrically determined outside the circumference, he succeeds in constructing with the compass alone all possible divisions of the circumference without even the error of one-sixth of a second; yet these are not the only constructions in the book.

The solution of any problem can be reduced to the determination of points, since even when the problem requires drawing a straight line or describing a circumference, the former is determined by two of its points and the latter by its center and a point on its periphery; and the required points are always obtained through the mutual intersection of straight lines and circumferences.
Making use of the two instruments, the straightedge and the compass, all problems are solved, since with the straightedge alone one determines the point of intersection of two straight lines, with the compass alone the points of intersection of two circumferences, and with both together the points of intersection of a straight line and a circumference.
