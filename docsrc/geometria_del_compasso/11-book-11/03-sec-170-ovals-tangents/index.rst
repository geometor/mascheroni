**170. Problem.** With center :math:`A` (Fig. 84), to describe a circle that touches the four circles last inscribed (§ 169) in a given circle.

*Solution.* Find a third proportional to the two straight lines :math:`FP, Fa` (§ 86). Taking this as radius, and with center :math:`A`, describe a circle. It will be the required one.

*Demonstration.* Since :math:`PF = 1` and :math:`Fa = \sqrt{2} - 1`, the third proportional will be

.. folio:: 0125
   :page: 100

equal to :math:`3 - 2\sqrt{2}`. Now let :math:`q` be the point where radius :math:`Ar` cuts the circle described with center :math:`R`. Then :math:`qr`, the diameter of that circle, will be :math:`= 2aF = 2\sqrt{2} - 2`;
which value subtracted from :math:`1 = Ar` will leave :math:`Aq = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}` equal to that third proportional. Therefore point :math:`q` will lie on both circumferences; and lying on straight line :math:`AR`, the perpendicular to this line at :math:`q` will be tangent to both circles, which will touch at :math:`q` (III.13 and 16). The same is proved for the others.

**171. Problem.** To find a circular arc whose cosine equals its chord (Fig. 85).

*Solution.* With radius :math:`AB` supposed :math:`= 1`, describe arc :math:`BCDE`, and make :math:`AB = BC = CD = DE = DP = CP`. Then make :math:`BD = Ba = Ea`, and :math:`Aa = BF`. With radius :math:`PF` and center :math:`B`, mark an arc cutting arc :math:`BC` at :math:`Q`. Arc :math:`BQ` will be the one required.

*Demonstration.* Points :math:`A, F, a, P` lie on the same straight line (§ 13). Moreover, points :math:`B, A, D, P`, being at distance :math:`CB` from point :math:`C`, lie on the circumference of a circle described with center :math:`C` and radius :math:`CB`. Since :math:`CB = BA = AD = DP`, :math:`BCP` will be a diameter of this circle (VI.15) and :math:`PA = \sqrt{3}` (§ 2).
Hence :math:`PF = \sqrt{3} - 1 = BQ`. If we now drop :math:`QR` perpendicular onto :math:`AB`, we shall have :math:`(BQ)^2 = (AB)^2 + (AQ)^2 - 2AB \cdot AR` (II.13); that is, :math:`(\sqrt{3} - 1)^2 = 4 - 2\sqrt{3} = 2 - 2AR`, and therefore :math:`AR = \sqrt{3} - 1 = BQ`, as was proposed to be done.

These last Problems are from Ozanam, solved by him with ruler and compass.

**172. Problem.** Given the axes :math:`BE, MN` (Fig. 86) of an ellipse, to describe around them an oval composed of four circular arcs tangent to one another.

*Solution.* With center :math:`A`, where the axes intersect, and with radius :math:`AB`, which is the semi-major axis, describe the

.. folio:: 0126
   :page: 101

semicircle :math:`BDE`, and make :math:`EA = ED`.
With center :math:`B` and radius :math:`BD`, cut axis :math:`BA` at :math:`d`. With center :math:`A` and radius :math:`AM`, cut semi-axis :math:`AE` at :math:`m`. With the same center :math:`A` and radius :math:`Ad`, describe arc :math:`de`, and make :math:`Em = de`. With an arbitrary radius and centers :math:`d` and :math:`e`, cut arc :math:`BDE` at :math:`\delta` and :math:`\varepsilon`.
With radius :math:`\delta\varepsilon` and center :math:`A`, cut axis :math:`BE` at :math:`P` and :math:`Q`. With centers :math:`P` and :math:`Q` and radius :math:`PB = QE`, describe arcs :math:`FBf, GEg`, and make :math:`PB = BF = Bf = EG = Eg`. Next make :math:`PQ = PR = Pr = QR = Qr`. Then with center :math:`R` and radius :math:`RF`, describe arc :math:`FG`, which will pass through :math:`M`. With the same radius :math:`RF = rf` and center :math:`r`, describe arc :math:`fg`, which will pass through :math:`N`, and what was desired will be done.

.. picture:: 0126.g1.png
   :alt: Archival Fig. 86 (folio 0126, p. 101): Four-center basket-handle oval constructed on prescribed axes (§ 172)

*Demonstration.* Since triangles :math:`BFP, PQR` are equilateral, angles :math:`BPF, QPR` are equal (I.8); hence the two lines :math:`FP, PR` will form a single straight line, because the two angles :math:`FPA + APR` equal the two :math:`FPA + FPB` (I.13 and 14). Therefore the two arcs :math:`BF, FG` will touch one another at :math:`F` (III.13).
The same contact is proved at points :math:`f, G, g`. Now if we suppose for brevity :math:`AB = 1`, we have :math:`BD = \sqrt{3}` (§ 2) :math:`= Bd`; hence :math:`Ad = \sqrt{3} - 1`. Since :math:`Ad : de :: A\delta : \delta\varepsilon` (§ 93), multiplying both terms of the first ratio by :math:`\sqrt{3} + 1` (V.4) gives :math:`Ad(\sqrt{3} + 1) : de(\sqrt{3} + 1) :: A\delta : \delta\varepsilon`,
and substituting the numerical values of :math:`Ad` and :math:`A\delta`, and carrying out the multiplication in the first term, we obtain :math:`2 : de(\sqrt{3} + 1) :: 1 : \delta\varepsilon`. Hence :math:`2\delta\varepsilon = 2AP = de(\sqrt{3} + 1) = PQ = PR`. Since :math:`PRQr` is a rhombus, we shall have

.. folio:: 0127
   :page: 102

(§ 139) :math:`(Rr)^2 = 4(PR)^2 - (PQ)^2 = 3(PR)^2`.
Hence :math:`Rr = PR \cdot \sqrt{3} = de(3 + \sqrt{3})`, and :math:`RA = \frac{1}{2}de(3 + \sqrt{3})`. Also :math:`AM = Am = AE - Em = AE - de = 1 - de`. Hence :math:`RA + AM = 1 + \frac{1}{2}de(1 + \sqrt{3})`, that is, :math:`RM = 1 + \frac{1}{2}PR`. Since :math:`PF = PB = 1 - AP = 1 - \frac{1}{2}PR`, we get :math:`PF + PR = 1 + \frac{1}{2}PR`, i.e. :math:`FR = RM`. Thus arc :math:`FG` passes through :math:`M`, and likewise arc :math:`fg` passes through :math:`N`. Q.E.D.

**173. Problem.** To describe (Fig. 87) a spiral :math:`BLEMFNGPH` composed of several circular arcs.

*Solution.* Let :math:`BE = BF` be the distance we wish to give to the revolutions of this spiral. Having bisected :math:`BE` at :math:`A` (§ 66), with center :math:`A` and radius :math:`AB`, describe the semicircle :math:`BLE` (§ 64). With center :math:`B` and radius :math:`BE`, describe semicircle :math:`EMF`.
Again with center :math:`A` and radius :math:`AF`, describe semicircle :math:`FNG`. Again with center :math:`B` and radius :math:`BG`, describe semicircle :math:`GPH`. In the same manner this spiral could be continued indefinitely.

In the same way this spiral can be doubled if, taking point :math:`b` at any desired distance from :math:`B` on :math:`AB` (§ 73), with centers :math:`A` and :math:`b` alternately we describe semicircles :math:`ble, emf, fng, gph`, etc.

This Problem needs no demonstration.

**174. Problem.** To find :math:`\sqrt{\sqrt{2}}` and :math:`\sqrt{\sqrt{3}}`.

*Solution.* With radius :math:`AB = 1` and center :math:`A` (Fig. 88), describe semicircle :math:`BCDE`, making :math:`AB = BC = CD = DE`. Then make :math:`BD = Ba = Ea`; next :math:`Aa = BP` and :math:`AB = EP`. On the semicircle mark points :math:`H, I, K`, making :math:`AB = aH = HI = IK`.

.. folio:: 0128
   :page: 103

With radius :math:`AP` and centers :math:`E` and :math:`H`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`L` and :math:`M`. Then :math:`LM = \sqrt{\sqrt{2}}`.

With radius :math:`AB` and centers :math:`a` and :math:`K`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`Q` and :math:`R`. Then :math:`QR = \sqrt{\sqrt{3}}`.

.. picture:: 0128.g1.png
   :alt: Archival Fig. 88 (folio 0128, p. 103): Tangent-arc curve and curvature transition

*Demonstration.* Since :math:`ELHM` is a rhombus, we have (§ 139) :math:`(LM)^2 = 4(LH)^2 - (HE)^2 = 4(AP)^2 - (HE)^2`; but :math:`(AP)^2 = \frac{1}{2}` (§ 104) and :math:`(HE)^2 = 2 - \sqrt{2}` (§§ 30 and 36). Hence :math:`(LM)^2 = \sqrt{2}`, and :math:`LM = \sqrt{\sqrt{2}}`.

Likewise :math:`aQKR` is a rhombus, so :math:`(QR)^2 = 4(aQ)^2 - (aK)^2`, with :math:`(aQ)^2 = (AB)^2 = 1` and :math:`(aK)^2 = 4 - \sqrt{3}` (§ 161); hence :math:`(QR)^2 = \sqrt{3}` and :math:`QR = \sqrt{\sqrt{3}}`.

**175.** With similar devices one could find the fourth roots of the other whole numbers without using the method of finding mean proportionals (§ 99). By that method, to obtain :math:`\sqrt{\sqrt{2}}`, one would have had to find a mean proportional between :math:`1` and :math:`\sqrt{2}`, or between :math:`\frac{1}{2}` and :math:`2\sqrt{2}`,
or between two other quantities whose product gave :math:`\sqrt{2}`. Yet its path would be far more complicated. If this Geometry of the Compass is cultivated, very useful fruits will come of it. I have prepared further researches upon this subject, which may find place in a larger Work than this. Here is a use of the preceding Problem with respect to the regular tetrahedral pyramid.

**176. Problem.** Given the edge :math:`AB` (Fig. 89) of a regular tetrahedral pyramid :math:`SABC`, to find: :math:`1^\circ` its altitude; :math:`2^\circ` the side of a square equal to its surface; :math:`3^\circ` the side of a square upon which, constructing a pyramid whose altitude is the edge of the proposed one, it equals it in volume;
:math:`4^\circ` the side of a square upon which, constructing a pyramid of altitude equal to the proposed one, it also equals it in volume; :math:`5^\circ` the radius of a circumscribed sphere.

*Solution.* Having constructed Fig. 88 with radius :math:`AB` as in Problem

.. folio:: 0129
   :page: 104

§ 174, with center :math:`B` and radius :math:`Ba`, describe arc :math:`aN`, and make :math:`Aa = EN`.
With radius :math:`AN` and centers :math:`A` and :math:`E`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`n`. With the same radius :math:`nA` and center :math:`n`, cut semicircle :math:`BCDE` at :math:`S`. Bisect :math:`LM` at :math:`m` (§ 66), and likewise :math:`QR` at :math:`q`. There will be:

:math:`1^\circ` :math:`BS` the altitude of the pyramid.

:math:`2^\circ` :math:`QR` the side of the square equal to its surface.

:math:`3^\circ` :math:`Mm` the side of the square base of a pyramid of altitude equal to the edge of the proposed one, and equal to it in volume.

:math:`4^\circ` :math:`Qq` the side of the square base of a pyramid of altitude and volume equal to the proposed one.

:math:`5^\circ` :math:`AN` the diameter of a circumscribed sphere.

*Demonstration.* If from the vertex :math:`S` of the pyramid a perpendicular :math:`Sm` is dropped onto edge :math:`AB`, since triangle :math:`SAB` is equilateral it will bisect :math:`AB` at :math:`m` (I.12). If then in base :math:`ABC` the perpendicular :math:`mT` is raised to :math:`AB` from :math:`m`, it will pass through :math:`C` (I.11),
and point :math:`T`, where the perpendicular from :math:`S` falls onto the base, will lie upon it (XI.11). Likewise it is proved that point :math:`T` lies on line :math:`Bn`, which bisects side :math:`AC`. Draw :math:`mn`; it will be parallel to :math:`BC` (VI.2), and triangle :math:`Amn` will be equiangular to triangle :math:`ABC` (I.27),
and :math:`BC` double :math:`mn` (VI.4). Triangles :math:`BCT, mnT` will also be equiangular to one another (I.15 and 27), and :math:`BC : mn :: CT : mT` (VI.4). Thus :math:`CT` is double :math:`mT`; hence :math:`mT = \frac{1}{3}Cm`. Now setting :math:`AB = 1`, we have :math:`Cm = Sm = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{3}` (§ 104). Thus :math:`Tm = \frac{1}{6}\sqrt{3}`.
Since :math:`(Sm)^2 = (mT)^2 + (ST)^2` (I.47), i.e., :math:`\frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{12} + (ST)^2`, we obtain :math:`(ST)^2 = \frac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}`, and hence :math:`ST = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}`. Also (Fig. 88) :math:`AN = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6}` (§ 104) :math:`= \sqrt{\frac{6}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} = An`, and :math:`An : AS :: AS : SB` (§ 86), i.e., :math:`\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} : 1 :: 1 : SB`; thus :math:`SB = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} = ST` (Fig. 89), which was the first point.

The surface of the tetrahedral pyramid is equal to four times the surface of its base (Fig. 89) :math:`ABC`, which

.. folio:: 0130
   :page: 105

being equal to :math:`\frac{1}{2}AB \cdot Cm = \frac{1}{4}\sqrt{3}` (I.41), the surface of the pyramid will be :math:`= \sqrt{3}`. Hence the side of the square equal to it :math:`= \sqrt{\sqrt{3}}`. But in Fig. 88, :math:`QR = \sqrt{\sqrt{3}}` (§ 174), so :math:`QR` is the required side, which was the second point.

Since in equal pyramids the bases and altitudes are reciprocally proportional to one another (XII.9), we shall have :math:`1 : \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} :: \frac{1}{4}\sqrt{3}` to the base of the pyramid whose altitude is :math:`= AB = 1`. Hence the area of this base will be :math:`= \frac{1}{4}\sqrt{2}`, and the side of the square of this area :math:`= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sqrt{2}} = Mm` (§ 174). Which was the third point.

Since pyramids of equal altitude are in the ratio of their bases, the square equal to triangle :math:`ABC = \frac{1}{4}\sqrt{3}` will have side :math:`\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sqrt{3}} = Qq` (§ 173). Which was the fourth point.

Furthermore, the diameter of the sphere circumscribed about the tetrahedral pyramid is sesquialter in power of the side of the pyramid (XIII.13), i.e., :math:`= \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}`, which is therefore :math:`= AN`. Which was the fifth point.

**177. Problem.** Given the altitude :math:`ST` of a regular tetrahedral pyramid (Figs. 88 and 89), to find its edge :math:`AB`.

*Solution.* With a radius :math:`AB` (Fig. 88) equal to :math:`ST` (Fig. 89), describe semicircle :math:`BCDE` making :math:`AB = BC = CD = DE`. With center :math:`B` and radius :math:`BD`, describe arc :math:`DNa`, and with the same radius and center :math:`E`, cut it at :math:`a`.
With radius :math:`Aa` and the same center :math:`E`, cut it at :math:`N`. Then :math:`AN` will be the required edge equal to edge :math:`AB` of Fig. 89.

*Demonstration.* In Fig. 88 we have :math:`AB : AN :: 1 : \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}` (§ 176), and since :math:`1 : \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} :: \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} : 1`, as proved by equality of product of extremes and means, :math:`AB` is to :math:`AN` as :math:`\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}` to :math:`1`, the ratio of altitude to edge (§ 176). Q.E.D.

**178. Problem.** To divide (Fig. 90) :math:`AB = 1` into five equal parts even in the case where a quintuple of :math:`AB` cannot be had, as in § 69.

*Solution.* With radius :math:`AB` and center :math:`A`, having described circle :math:`BDp`, and made :math:`AB = BC = CD = DE` on its circumference, and then :math:`BD = Ba = B\alpha = Ea = E\alpha`,
with radius :math:`AB` and center :math:`\alpha`, cut the circumference at :math:`g`, and with the same radius and center :math:`g`, describe arc :math:`An\alpha`. Now with radius :math:`BE` and center :math:`a`, cut this arc at :math:`n`. With radius :math:`\alpha n` and center :math:`B`, cut the

.. folio:: 0131
   :page: 106

circumference at :math:`P` and :math:`p`. With the same radius :math:`pB` and centers :math:`p` and :math:`P`, mark two arcs intersecting at :math:`Q`. Then :math:`AQ` will be a fifth part of :math:`AB` laid along its direction.

*Demonstration.* If a point :math:`u` is supposed on the direction of :math:`AE`, with :math:`Au = Aa`, then by right angles :math:`aAu, \alpha Au`, we have :math:`(au)^2 = 2(aA)^2 = 2(\alpha A)^2 = (\alpha u)^2 = 4 = (BE)^2`; hence :math:`au = \alpha u = BE = an`. Since angles :math:`gAB, gA\alpha` are equal, both half-right (§ 30), angles :math:`gAa, gAu` are equal, each equal to three half-right angles.
Hence in the two triangles :math:`gAa, gAu`, having an equal angle included between equal sides, the third side :math:`ga` will equal the third :math:`gu` (I.4); so a circle described with center :math:`g` and radius :math:`ga` will pass through :math:`u` and be concentric to circle :math:`An\alpha`. Moreover :math:`ga = \sqrt{5}` (§ 104), and since :math:`an = u\alpha`, we have (§ 93) :math:`ga : gA :: au : n\alpha`,
that is, :math:`\sqrt{5} : 1 :: 2 : n\alpha`. Therefore :math:`n\alpha = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}`. Hence each side of rhombus :math:`PBpQ` is also :math:`= n\alpha = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}`. Thus comparing points :math:`P, B, p, Q, A` of this Fig. 90 with points :math:`A, p, B, P, Q` of Fig. 3, we have for Fig. 90 :math:`BQ \cdot BA = (BP)^2` (§ 19), i.e. :math:`BQ = \frac{4}{5}`. Hence :math:`AQ`, which lies in the same straight line (§ 13), :math:`= \frac{1}{5}`.
